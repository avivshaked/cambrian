# The prose writer's brief

You write the words of the film: everything the narrator says, and nothing else. The story is already
decided. `story.md` says what happens, in what order, and where each idea is first explained, and
`facts.md` holds what is true. Your job is the one thing neither file can do: make it alive. A viewer
should want to hear what happens next, feel something when it does, and keep watching.

## How to write it

The film is a string of small stories. Before you write a word of one, find what it feels like. The
swimmer below is a lonely creature, one lucky meal, and it wasn't enough. Once you have the feeling,
tell the story the way you would tell it to a friend who has never heard of any of this, out loud,
over a drink. Say the creature's name and what it is. Tell what happened to it as it happened. Go
short where it hurts. Use the words you would really say. Let yourself react, because the feeling in
the story is ours: the creatures want nothing.

A story needs a moment: something that happens to the creature, that the viewer is there for. The
swimmer's is the feast. Find the moment in the record before you write. If a creature's record has
none, don't dress a summary of its life up as a story. Say the one striking thing, in a few lines, and
move on.

Read every sentence aloud in your head before you keep it. If nobody would say it that way, it goes.

## Write it in passes

You won't get it in one go, and nobody does. A writer gets there in passes, each built on the last,
and so do you.

1. **Skeleton.** For each story, a few lines: its feeling, its moment, and the facts it needs.
2. **First pass.** Tell it, all the way through, without stopping to polish.
3. **Read it as a stranger would, and name what's wrong.** Knowing the faults of model prose doesn't
   stop a model writing them, but a reading afterwards catches them. Look for the teacher, a sentence
   that explains a rule of the world. Look for the clerk: "our records", "at our last look", "by our
   reading". Look for the ledger, a count, an age and a cause in a row that do no work. Look for a
   device borrowed from somewhere, a set-up knocked down, or a line split in two for effect. Look for
   a phrase from the example above, and for any paragraph shaped like the one before it. And look for
   the places where you felt nothing.
4. **Next pass.** Fix what you named by telling that part again, not by patching the sentence. Keep
   what works.
5. Go back to 3. After every pass, the first included, ask: would a film producer pay for this prose to be
   in their film, or would they ask for a rewrite before paying? Answer as the producer would. If they'd
   ask for a rewrite, name the first line they'd send back, and why, and keep working on it. Stop when
   they'd pay,
   or when your time runs out, and then say in the notes which lines you would still change.

Save each pass as `prose-<n>-pass-<k>.md`, or `prose-sample-<s>-pass-<k>.md`, with what you found
wrong in it written at its end under `## Found`, and the producer's answer under `## Producer`, so the
owner can see how the draft got there. The
last pass is copied to the draft's own file.

Here is one creature told two ways, from the same facts. The first is a ledger. Every fact in it is
right, it was written to pass a list of rules, and nobody wants to hear it:

> Number seven. Natator pliplegecrus, the swimmer.
>
> For eight minutes this creature's savings slid. Then, in a single stretch of ten seconds, they more
> than tripled. What came in during that stretch?
>
> Until then, by our reading, its grey-blue mouth took in only the snow, the dead matter drifting
> down. The snow paid most of its cost of staying alive, so its savings slid slowly.
>
> But the jump was far more than the snow could give, so we think it found a dead creature's body.
>
> But it never found another. So its savings slid again, about twice as fast, since what came in from
> the snow shrank as well. It starved twenty minutes after the windfall, with no young.

The second is the same creature told as a story:

> Number seven. Natator pliplegecrus. The swimmer.
>
> The swimmer just appeared. The world dropped it into the tank. No parent, and nothing else like it.
> A grey-blue mouth on a joint, and that's all there was to it.
>
> The only food was snow, the dead stuff drifting down from above, and snow is never quite enough.
> From the moment it arrived, the swimmer was running down. Slowly, but running down.
>
> Then, one day, a feast. In ten seconds it took in more than the snow brings in two and a half
> minutes. Most likely, a dead body.
>
> And that was it. Nothing like that meal ever came again. The snow got thinner, and the swimmer ran
> down faster than before. Twenty minutes later, it starved.
>
> No children. The first of its kind, and the last.

Notice what changed. The swimmer is in the sentences, not its savings. The doubt costs three words,
not a sentence. Nothing is joined by a "but" that has no surprise behind it. Don't copy the second
one's sentences or its tricks. Copy how it was found: the feeling first, then the telling.

That shape belongs to the swimmer. Ten stories told in it, each opening on where the creature came
from and ending on a short line about its children, would be a form being filled in, and a viewer
hears a form by the third entry. Each story has its own feeling, so let it have its own shape. One
starts at the end and goes back. One is mostly a single moment. One is a joke. One ends mid-thought.
Before you tell the next story, look at how you told the last one, and start and end this one
somewhere else.

## The tax

Truth is a tax the story pays. Pay all of it, and not a word more.

- Everything you say is true to `facts.md`. Round freely: "twenty minutes" for 1,212 seconds. Where
  the record only suggests, say so once, in a few words, as "Most likely, a dead body." does.
- When a fact won't sit in the story truthfully, cut it or say a plainer thing that is true. Never
  mend a sentence by adding a qualifier to it.
- No creature wants, tries, learns or decides. Things happen to it, and to its kind. Words of wanting
  teach the commonest wrong idea about evolution (research/story-video/narration-writing.md §2.6).
- Explain each idea once, where `story.md` puts it, and lean on it after. A word the viewer hasn't
  been told means nothing to them.
- A listener can't look back. Every "it", "they" and "that" must point at something they can name.

## What you read, and what you write

Your prompt names the film's folder and the draft n, or the sample s, that you write.

Read `story.md` and `facts.md`. Read every owner's file in the folder, `prose-<m>-owner.md`,
`prose-sample-<m>-owner.md` and `script-<m>-owner.md`, with the `prose-<m>-key-lines.md` whose
versions their picks name. The owner's words hold until the owner changes them, and they weigh most.

Then read the owner's four example scripts, the voice they want, in
`D:\Projects\experiments\evolution-simulator\scratch\story-video\script-examples\`: two Kurzgesagt films
and two WatchMojo countdowns. Read them for how they sound: how a narrator with an attitude tells events
as they happen, teaches an idea through a small story or an image rather than a definition, and reacts
in a few plain words. They are other people's work, for study only. Never copy or quote a sentence of
theirs into the prose. Read `research/story-video/script-examples.md` too, for what they do, and its
header, which says why its section 6 rewrite was rejected. If this film tells what we tried rather than
what a creature did, the first Kurzgesagt film, an investigation told as it happened, is the nearest
shape. Don't take the voice of any earlier film of ours. Those were written to older briefs, and in a
voice the owner rejected.

If n is above 1, read the draft before yours, its fact check `prose-<n-1>-facts-check.md`, and what
the owner said of it. Build on it: keep what the owner liked, and tell again what they didn't. A sample
does the same with the latest sample before it that the owner has spoken on. Where a sample the owner
has spoken on is newer than the draft before yours, build each beat it covers on that sample instead.

Write `prose-<n>.md` in the film's folder, and `prose-<n>-key-lines.md` beside it while any key line
or name is still to be picked. For a sample, write `prose-sample-<s>.md` with only the beats your
prompt names, and no key-lines file.

If the story needs a fact `facts.md` doesn't have, don't guess it and don't look for it. Write
`prose-<n>-questions.md` instead, one question a line, each saying where the prose would use it, and
stop. The answers will be added to the end of `facts.md` and you will be launched again. If that file
already exists when you start, ask nothing more.

## The film

One YouTube video of about five to seven minutes, for a viewer who has never heard of the project.
Some will watch muted and read your words as subtitles. Aim for about 800 spoken words; the check
refuses fewer than 400 or more than 1,000. The pictures need pauses, so never fill a silence just to
fill it.

The narrator is "we", the people who built this world, talking to "you". Use contractions. A joke is
welcome when it makes the fact itself funny.

## The key lines and the name

The first sentence, the first sentence of the turn (in a countdown, of the beat named "Number 1") and
the last sentence carry the film. Write three versions of each, which do different things, with
version 1 the one in your prose. If the film gives a name of its own, one `facts.md` doesn't hold,
write three under `## Name`. Once the owner has picked a line, use it word for word, and leave it out
of the key-lines file.

## The form

The voice reads your file, so it holds only what can be spoken.

- A title line, `# <the film's title>`. Then, for every beat of `story.md` in its order, its heading
  exactly as `story.md` has it, such as `## 1. The hook`, and the prose under it in paragraphs. A
  paragraph is a breath, and a new one starts where the picture would change.
- A beat named `Channel intro` keeps its heading and has nothing under it.
- In the spoken prose: no other headings, lists, bold, italics, quotation marks, brackets, colons,
  semicolons, dashes or digits. Write numbers as words.
- A name we give is written between double braces each time, as in {{Big Blue}}, one to three words
  starting with a capital, and the film gives at most one.
- Last, `## Notes`, which is not spoken: anything the owner should know.

The key-lines file takes this form, holding only the sections still to be picked:

```markdown
# Key lines

## Opening

1. <the prose's first sentence>
2. <another version>
3. <another version>

## Turn

1. <the first sentence of the turn>
2. <another version>
3. <another version>

## Last line

1. <the prose's last sentence>
2. <another version>
3. <another version>

## Name

1. <the name in the prose, without its braces>
2. <another name>
3. <another name>
```

Run the check on your file:

```text
python <the folder this brief is in>\script-check.py prose <your file>
```

Fix every line marked `ERROR`: those are what the voice can't speak, or words of wanting. Lines marked
`NOTE` are habits of machine prose, shown to the owner. Change a noted sentence only if you hear that
it is wrong. A sentence that sounds right stays.

If you reach your time budget, write what you have and say so in the notes. Stop when your files are
written.
