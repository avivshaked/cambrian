# Writing the words of a short narrated science video

Research notes for the story videos: short narrated science stories for social media, each telling what happened in one experiment of an artificial-life simulator, with footage of the simulated creatures, narration, subtitles and graphics. The notes cover writing for the ear, the narrator's voice and tone, how the words work with the picture, subtitles and on-screen text, narration held up as excellent, and the faults that lose listeners. They end with the ten best-supported rules and a list of every source.

Compiled on 2026-09-26 from web searches and the pages they led to. The notes are meant to be used later without going back to the sources, so each source is described in full: what it is and who made it, what it studied and how, what it found, its limits, and what it means for our narration. No file in the repository was read to write them.

Contents: 0. How to read these notes. 1. Writing for the ear. 2. Narration voice and tone for science. 3. How narration works with the picture. 4. Subtitles and on-screen text. 5. Narration held up as excellent, and what its writing does. 6. Common faults that lose or confuse listeners. 7. Tensions, gaps and open questions. 8. The ten most useful, best-supported rules. 9. Source list.

## 0. How to read these notes

**Source types.** Each source carries one tag. [Study] is one experiment, survey or corpus analysis, peer-reviewed unless said otherwise. [Meta-analysis] is a statistical synthesis of many studies. [Review] is a narrative review, or a theory paper that gathers evidence. [Platform data] is numbers a platform, an ad agency or a media company published about its own users, usually a self-report survey or an internal test whose method is not disclosed; it shows direction more than size, the publisher has an interest in the answer, and almost all of it concerns advertising rather than science video. [Guideline] is a broadcaster's or captioning body's house standard: codified practice, partly informed by research, but not itself an experiment. [Practitioner] is advice from working journalists, documentary makers, voice actors and trainers: opinion distilled from experience. [Commentary] is description or analysis by a critic, a journalist or a fan site.

**How each source was checked.** "Read" means the page itself was fetched and read. "Abstract" means only the abstract, a database record or a search engine's summary of the paper was seen, not the full text. Several publishers (SAGE, Taylor and Francis, PNAS's own site, CBE-LSE, the BBC, Poynter's book pages, Fast Company) refused the fetch with HTTP 403, so a good share of the studies here rest on their abstracts, and their numbers are as the abstract gives them. "Snippet" means the only basis is a search engine's summary of the page. That is the weakest footing, and it is flagged wherever a claim rests on it.

**Evidence labels.** EVIDENCE means supported by at least one controlled study or meta-analysis. PLATFORM means supported by platform data only. PRACTICE means practitioner consensus with no experiment behind it that I found. GUIDELINE means a published house standard. INFERENCE means my own reasoning from the sources to our case; it is never presented as a finding.

**One caveat applies throughout.** Most controlled studies here measure recall or comprehension in a laboratory: students, instructional animations, television news clips, radio bulletins, often with people who expect a test. Someone scrolling a feed is not that viewer. The direction of a finding probably carries over to a short social video; its effect size probably does not. Several studies are in other languages (Rodero's radio work is in Spanish) or on other populations (children, second-language listeners, older listeners with hearing loss), and that is said where it matters. Nothing I found tests the narration of short science films on social media directly.

## 1. Writing for the ear

### 1.1 The premise: a listener cannot go back

A reader who loses the thread of a sentence can read it again. A listener hears it once, at the speaker's pace, and in a video is also watching the picture and perhaps reading subtitles. Nearly all the practitioner advice in this section follows from that premise. The laboratory evidence that bears on it is about how little a listener can hold at once, and how much it costs to hold it.

**Cowan, "The magical number 4 in short-term memory" (2001).** [Review; abstract] Nelson Cowan's target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences (24(1): 87-114) gathered evidence on memory capacity from verbal and non-verbal material, visual and auditory presentation, single and dual tasks, and attended and unattended stimuli. He argued that Miller's famous "seven plus or minus two" was a rough rhetorical figure, and that the real limit of the focus of attention is about four chunks in adults. It is among the most cited papers in memory research. Its limit for our purpose is that a "chunk" depends on what the listener already knows, so the number is not a word count. What it means for us: a sentence that asks a listener to hold several new things before they can be put together (a subject, two qualifications, a number, and only then the verb) can exceed what the listener holds, and there is no second pass. Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496/S0140525X01003922a.pdf/the-magical-number-4-in-short-term-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity.pdf

**Gibson, dependency locality theory (1998 onward).** [Review/theory; abstract] Edward Gibson's theory (Cognition, 1998, "Linguistic complexity: locality of syntactic dependencies") says that understanding a sentence carries two memory costs. Storage is the cost of keeping incomplete structure open, such as a subject waiting for its verb. Integration is the cost of linking a new word back to one said earlier, and it grows with the number of new things mentioned in between. The theory explains why centre-embedded clauses and object-extracted relative clauses are hard, and later work (Futrell, Levy and Gibson 2020) argues that languages order their words to keep dependents close. The evidence is mostly reading times, so it concerns readers, who can look back. What it means for us (INFERENCE): the cost can only be larger for a listener. Keep the subject and its verb together and early, and put qualifications after the main clause rather than inside it. Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010027798000341 ; https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Futrell_Levy_Gibson_2020.pdf

**"Syntactic complexity differentially affects auditory sentence comprehension performance for individuals with age-related hearing loss" (Frontiers in Psychology, 2023).** [Study; abstract] The study compared older adults with age-related hearing loss against controls on spoken sentences whose difficulty was varied either by voice (active or passive) or by length (three or four phrases). With length held constant, the listeners with hearing loss did worse on passive sentences than on active ones, compared with controls, and working memory capacity was the strongest predictor of how well passives were understood. Its limit is the population. What it means for us: active voice is safer by ear, especially for the listeners whose hearing or attention is not at its best, and in a noisy feed that is a larger share of the audience than in a laboratory. Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1264994/full

**Gunter, Poor Reception: Misunderstanding and Forgetting Broadcast News (1987).** [Review; abstract] Barrie Gunter's book is described by its publisher as the most comprehensive review of the research on the cognitive side of television news, drawing on studies in the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Sweden, Finland and Israel. I did not obtain its figures, so it is cited here only as the standard review behind the claim that broadcast news is poorly understood and quickly forgotten. Source: https://www.routledge.com/Poor-Reception-Misunderstanding-and-Forgetting-Broadcast-News/Gunter/p/book/9780805810103

### 1.2 One idea per sentence

**NPR Training, "The journey from print to radio storytelling".** [Practitioner; snippet, the page timed out twice] NPR's training site, written for print reporters moving to radio, says that radio sentences must be far shorter and that information must unfurl more slowly, because "our ears can only handle one fact or idea per sentence". It calls dependent clauses "toxic" to radio writing, on the grounds that people rarely use them in conversation, and shows one long sentence about a politician's charity pledge rewritten as four short sentences carrying the same information. The same site's "Campfire tales: the essentials of writing for radio" covers similar ground (not read). The limit is that this is house practice, not an experiment; its weight is that it is the practice of the organisation most associated with narrated audio storytelling. What it means for us: plan the narration as a sequence of single ideas, each in its own sentence. Sources: https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-65814/the-journey-from-print-to-radio-storytelling-a-guide-for-navigating-a-new-landscape (older address https://training.npr.org/2017/12/06/the-journey-from-print-to-radio-storytelling-a-guide-for-navigating-a-new-landscape/) ; https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/31/g-s1-65875/campfire-tales-the-essentials-of-writing-for-radio

**Numerical targets from journalism courses.** [Practitioner; snippet] A University of Arkansas journalism guide (as summarised by search; the page refused the fetch) recommends sentences of 20 words or fewer with one idea each, and a journalism training site gives an average of 15 to 20 words. These are rules of thumb; I found no study that derives them. Sources: https://uark.pressbooks.pub/journalismgsp/chapter/broadcast/ ; https://journalism.university/broadcast-and-online-journalism/language-differences-radio-print/

**The read-aloud test.** [Practitioner; read and snippet] Every radio guide found recommends reading the script aloud before it is final. Media Helping Media, a training site published with the Fojo Media Institute at Linnaeus University in Sweden, puts it this way: "Read your script aloud: Before you finish, read your work out loud to check for any tongue-twisters or sentences that are too long to say in one breath." NPR's guidance (snippet) adds that a script is not finished until it flows off the tongue. Source: https://mediahelpingmedia.org/quick-guides/writing-a-radio-news-script/

**What the evidence does and does not say.** No controlled study I found sets a number of words per sentence for spoken narration. The support for short, single-idea sentences is indirect: the small working memory in Cowan, the cost of distance in Gibson, and the active-passive result in the hearing-loss study. So the direction (fewer ideas per sentence, subject and verb early and close, no clause held open) is EVIDENCE, indirectly, and any particular count (15 words, 20 words) is PRACTICE. INFERENCE: the rule is about structure, not about making every sentence short. A run of very short sentences of the same length sounds clipped when read aloud, and I found no study of rhythm in narration either way. The read-aloud test is the only check anyone recommends.

### 1.3 Word order, register and punctuation

**Block, Writing Broadcast News: Shorter, Sharper, Stronger.** [Practitioner; snippet] Mervin Block's handbook, a standard text for American broadcast newswriters, argues that in conversation we put the attribution first ("Jim told me ...") and follow subject, verb, object, so broadcast copy should do the same. Writers should "write the way you talk" and have the courage to use short words and short sentences. A sentence should not open with a participial phrase, because the listener then hears secondary information that means nothing until the subject arrives, and has to rearrange the pieces afterwards. Block's rule about openers and Gibson's dependency locality describe the same cost from two sides (INFERENCE). What it means for us: "Researchers found" goes before the finding; "the creatures" goes before what happened to them; no sentence opens with "Having spent a thousand generations in the dark, ...". Sources: https://www.worldradiohistory.com/BOOKSHELF-ARH/News/Writing-Broadcast-News-Block.pdf ; https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/467616.Writing_Broadcast_News

**Radio scripting guides on negatives, register, acronyms and punctuation.** [Practitioner; read and snippet] A journalism training site advises against negative constructions, because they make the listener reverse the meaning: "The union doesn't accept the proposal" is harder to take in than "The union rejects the proposal". It recommends contractions ("it's", "don't", "you're"), says that if you would not say it in conversation you should not put it in a script, tells writers to give an acronym in full and keep reminding the listener what it stands for, and notes that brackets and quotation marks are not audible. Media Helping Media (read) adds: "Use only commas and full stops. Do not use semi-colons or colons, as they can confuse the flow of your reading", and "Always say 'Prime Minister xxx' rather than 'xxx, the Prime Minister'." What it means for us: a colon or a bracket in a narration script marks a sentence written for the eye, and it should be recast. Say what a thing is before its name ("a simulated sea called ..." rather than "..., a simulated sea"). Sources: https://journalism.university/broadcast-and-online-journalism/scripting-radio-engaging-broadcasts-blueprint/ ; https://www.radiocult.fm/radio-bootcamp/writing-for-radio-script-guide ; https://mediahelpingmedia.org/quick-guides/writing-a-radio-news-script/

**Ginns, Martin and Marsh, "Designing instructional text in a conversational style: a meta-analysis" (2013).** [Meta-analysis; abstract] Published in Educational Psychology Review (25(4): 445-472), this synthesis pooled experiments on the "personalization principle": instruction rewritten with first- or second-person address, sentences that speak directly to the learner, polite forms, and the author's views and personality made visible. The pooled effects were reliable for retention (d = .30) and transfer (d = .54), and for learners' ratings of friendliness (d = .46) and of how well they processed the material (d = .45). They were not reliable for interest (d = .15) or for perceived learning assistance (d = .16). Limits: lessons, mostly students, outcomes measured by tests. What it means for us: a conversational, direct register is not only a matter of taste; in lessons it improved what people remembered and could apply, by a small to moderate amount. It did not reliably make them more interested, so a conversational voice is no substitute for a story. Sources: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-013-9228-0 ; https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1036785

### 1.4 Plain words

Block's advice to use short words (above) is the only direct practitioner source I found on vocabulary for narration, and the acronym rule above the only one on jargon. I found no study on word choice in spoken narration specifically. INFERENCE: word frequency is one of the most robust effects in reading research (Liao and colleagues, section 4.2, found long and rare words drew more attention from subtitle readers), and a listener cannot stop to look a word up, so a rare word costs more by ear than on the page. A technical term the story needs (a generation, a lineage, a mutation) is defined once in plain words the first time it is said, and then used unchanged.

### 1.5 Repetition, signposting and recaps

**Schneider, Beege, Nebel and Rey, "A meta-analysis of how signaling affects learning with media" (2018).** [Meta-analysis; abstract] In Educational Research Review (23: 1-24), the authors pooled 103 studies with 12,201 participants from 46 years of research on signalling: cues that highlight how material is organised or which part is relevant, such as headings, spoken markers, highlighting and arrows. Signalling improved retention and transfer, reduced cognitive load, and affected motivation, learning time and eye fixations; learners' prior knowledge did not moderate the effect. Two other meta-analyses of signalling exist, which I did not read: Alpizar, Adesope and Wong (2020, Educational Technology Research and Development) and Richter, Scheiter and Eitel (2016, on signalling the relations between text and pictures). What it means for us: short spoken signposts ("here is the surprise", "this is where it changed") and visual cues on the footage (an arrow, a highlight on one creature) are backed by a large body of evidence. Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X17300581 ; https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7.pdf ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/287807075_Signaling_text-picture_relations_in_multimedia_learning_A_comprehensive_meta-analysis

**Chaudron and Richards, "The effect of discourse markers on the comprehension of lectures" (1986).** [Study; abstract] In Applied Linguistics (7(2): 113-127), the authors made four versions of an American history lecture for foreign university students: a baseline with no special signals of organisation; one with micro-markers (fillers and links between sentences, such as "so", "well", "and then"); one with macro-markers (signals of the overall organisation, such as "what I'm going to talk about now is ..."); and one with both. Comprehension was tested by a cloze recall test, multiple choice and true-false. The version with macro-markers produced better recall than the version with micro-markers and the version with both. Limits: second-language listeners, a lecture several minutes long, 1986. What it means for us: the signposts that help are those that say where the story is going, not connective filler. A one-minute film has room for one or two. Sources: https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/7/2/113/163715 ; https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED259565

**Son, Reese and Davie, "Effects of visual-verbal redundancy and recaps on television news learning" (1987).** [Study; read, ERIC abstract] In the Journal of Broadcasting and Electronic Media (31: 207-216), 80 people watched a five-story newscast in a 2 x 2 design: pictures that matched the words or did not, with or without a spoken recap. Matching pictures and words produced more recall than non-matching ones, and in the abstract's words, "Analysis of the results showed the superiority of visual-verbal redundancy over recapping." Limits: 1980s television news, a small sample. What it means for us: making each claim land on the picture that shows it does more than summarising at the end. A closing sentence that restates the finding is cheap, but it is no substitute. Sources: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED270764 ; https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08838158709386658

**INFERENCE on repetition.** Repeat the key noun rather than switching to a pronoun or a synonym, because a listener who missed which "it" is meant cannot look back. Avoid "the former", "the latter", "respectively" and "as mentioned above", which all ask the listener to look back. I found no study of these forms; the inference rests on the premise in 1.1.

### 1.6 Numbers and units spoken aloud

**Broadcast rounding rules.** [Practitioner; read and snippet] NPR's training guide (snippet) says that "ears really hate numbers", that they should be kept to an absolute minimum, and that large figures should be rounded unless an exact figure is required: "nearly one-and-a-half million dollars", not 1,456,789, because the listener has forgotten the first digit by the time the last is said. Media Helping Media (read): "Simplify numbers: Do not use long, exact figures. Instead of saying '1,988 people', say 'nearly two thousand people'." A broadcast newswriting site describes the "two digits only" rule: reduce every number to two significant digits so that it does not tax short-term memory ("nearly 500" for 497, "about half" for 51 per cent, "six point eight" for 6.83, "roughly 530" for 527), and notes that numbers also take time to say. Sources: https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-65814/the-journey-from-print-to-radio-storytelling-a-guide-for-navigating-a-new-landscape ; https://mediahelpingmedia.org/quick-guides/writing-a-radio-news-script/ ; http://newscript.com/numbers.html (its certificate had expired when fetched; content via search) ; https://bardofbreakingnewsblog.wordpress.com/2020/09/21/the-best-way-to-express-numbers-in-broadcast-news/

**Barrio, Goldstein and Hofman, "Improving comprehension of numbers in the news" (2016).** [Study; abstract] Presented at CHI 2016 (the ACM conference on human factors in computing), the paper proposes "perspectives": short clauses that re-express a measurement through a ratio, a rank or a change of unit, for example by relating an unfamiliar quantity to a familiar one. In three randomised online experiments with more than 3,200 participants, adding a perspective substantially improved people's recall of measurements they had read, their estimates of related measurements they had not seen, and their ability to detect a measurement that had been altered. A follow-up (Riederer, Hofman and Goldstein, CHI 2018) generated such analogies automatically. Limits: the numbers were read on a screen, not heard, and the tasks were online surveys. What it means for us: a number in the narration earns its place when it comes with a comparison the viewer can picture ("about ten times as many as at the start"; "in about the time it takes one of them to grow up"). Sources: https://dangoldstein.com/papers/Barrio_Goldstein_Hofman_Improving_Comprehension_Numbers_News_CHI16.pdf ; https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Improving-Comprehension-of-Numbers-in-the-News-Barrio-Goldstein/a2fa9553d57f2d07ba04baa87132801470eb8cd6 ; https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3173574.3174122

**van der Bles, van der Linden, Freeman and Spiegelhalter, "The effects of communicating uncertainty on public trust in facts and numbers" (2020).** [Study; abstract] In PNAS, the authors ran five experiments with 5,780 people in total, including a preregistered replication on a national sample and a field experiment on the BBC News website. They varied the topic (for example global warming and immigration), the format of the uncertainty (verbal, or a numeric range) and its size. Stating uncertainty made people perceive more uncertainty, as intended, but caused only a small decrease in trust in the number and in its source, and mostly when the uncertainty was verbal rather than numeric. Limits: written news figures, not narration. What it means for us: a range ("between two and three hundred") or a plain statement of a limit costs the narrator little trust, and a vague verbal hedge costs a little more than a number would. Sources: https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1913678117 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32205438/

**Units.** I found no study or guideline on how units should be spoken. INFERENCE: say a unit in the word a listener uses ("metres", "seconds", "generations"), never as a symbol or an abbreviation, and translate a simulation's internal units into something the viewer can picture, such as a body length, a lifetime or a day of simulated time. That is the perspective finding applied to units. Likewise, one number per sentence is the one-idea rule applied to numbers; the sources above state it as practice, and nothing tests it.

### 1.7 How fast, and how much

**Rodero, "Influence of speech rate and information density on recognition: the moderate dynamic mechanism" (2016).** [Study; read through a research digest, abstract seen] Emma Rodero of Pompeu Fabra University in Barcelona published this in Media Psychology (19(2)). Two hundred communication students heard one-minute Spanish radio news items in five categories (sports, culture, society, national, international), read at 150 words a minute (slow), 170 to 190 (normal), 210 (fast) and 230 (very fast), with the information density varied. Recognition was best at the normal rate "regardless of density". The hardest combinations were mismatched extremes: low-density items at 150 words a minute and high-density items at 210. At 230, "the listener is incapable of maintaining and processing the incoming information". Rodero's model, the "moderate dynamic mechanism", is that the faster the speech, the lower the density must be, and the reverse: slow, sparse delivery loses the listener as surely as fast, dense delivery. She notes that most authors on radio recommend 160 to 180 words a minute, and says the findings "may have significant repercussions for newscasters and radio stations". Limits: Spanish, whose words carry more syllables on average than English ones, so the figures do not transfer one for one; newsreading without pictures; students. What it means for us: set pace and density together. A dense sentence (a number, a new term) wants a slower read and room after it; a light connecting sentence can move. A film that is slow and says little is not easier to follow; it loses attention. Sources: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15213269.2014.1002942 ; open copy https://repositori.upf.edu/bitstream/handle/10230/35734/Rodero_med_infl.pdf?sequence=1 ; digest https://journalistsresource.org/media/radio-news-pace-words/ ; https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Influence-of-Speech-Rate-and-Information-Density-on-Rodero/e1a69948fc9183593b7b6bf6b9a92809aaf7eea8

**Foulke and Sticht, review of compressed speech (1969).** [Review; abstract, through a later paper] The classic review of time-compressed speech, in Psychological Bulletin, reported that comprehension declines slowly as the rate rises, up to roughly 250 to 275 words a minute in English, and much faster beyond. Limits: old; laboratory listening to passages. Source (a later paper reporting the finding): https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03199702.pdf

**Murphy, Hoover, Agadzhanyan, Kuehn and Castel, "Learning in double time: the effect of lecture video speed on immediate and delayed comprehension" (2022).** [Study; abstract] In Applied Cognitive Psychology, students watched recorded lectures at normal speed, 1.5x, 2x and faster, and were tested immediately and after a delay of about a week. Going from 1x to 1.5x or 2x cost little comprehension; beyond 2x, performance fell. Limits: motivated students, lectures they expected to be tested on, and no competing footage to watch. What it means for us, together with the Foulke review (INFERENCE): at any normal narration rate, whether the words can be heard and understood is not the constraint. The constraint in a narrated film is how much attention is left for the picture, and how fast the subtitles must be read (section 4). Sources: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3899 ; https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/01/220111153637.htm

**Voice-over rates in practice.** [Practitioner; snippet] Voice-over script calculators and voice-actor guides agree on rough figures: documentary narration around 130 words a minute; long-form narration around 140, dropping to 130 with room for graphics and transitions or rising to 160 to 170 for a fast script; conversational narration 140 to 160; commercials 170 to 180; dramatic narration 120 to 130. These describe what the trade does; they are not tests of what works. Sources: https://www.vowordcounter.com/articles/understanding-wpm-by-genre/ ; https://www.voices.com/tools/words_to_time_conversion ; https://thevoiceoverguy.com.au/words-to-minutes

### 1.8 The voice that reads it

**Rodero, "The principle of distinctive and contrastive coherence of prosody in radio news: an analysis of perception and recognition" (2015).** [Study; abstract] In the Journal of Nonverbal Behavior, Rodero tested combinations of intonation in radio news and found that prosody following a principle of "distinctive and contrastive coherence" (pitch varied to mark what is new and important, and applied consistently) gave the most positive perception and the highest recognition. Limits: Spanish radio news. What it means for us: if the narration is synthesised, the engine's intonation matters to what is remembered. The script can help by putting the new and important word where stress falls naturally in English, at the end of the sentence (INFERENCE). Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10919-014-0201-5

**Rodero and Cores-Sarria, "Best prosody for news: a psychophysiological study comparing a broadcast to a narrative speaking style" (2023).** [Study; abstract, results not seen] In Communication Research (50(3): 361-384), 56 people heard six news items read in the typical "broadcast" style (emphatic intonation, fast rate) and in a narrative style, in a male and a female voice, with self-report and physiological measures. The authors note that the broadcast style had been criticised as repetitive and regular with no data to support the criticism. I did not obtain the results, so I record only that the comparison exists. Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/00936502211059360

**Craig and Schroeder, "Reconsidering the voice effect when learning from a virtual human" (2017), and "Text-to-speech software and learning: investigating the relevancy of the voice effect" (2019).** [Studies; abstract] The older "voice principle" in multimedia learning held that people learn better from a human voice than from a machine one. In a randomised trial with an online sample (Computers and Education, 2017), a virtual human presented a lesson in a classic text-to-speech voice, a modern one, or a recorded human voice. The modern engine produced more learning on transfer and better training efficiency than the old engine, and learners rated it as credible and as helpful as the human voice; the authors say this calls the older results into question. The 2019 paper in the Journal of Educational Computing Research pursued the same question (not read). Limits: the engines date from 2017 to 2019, and a lesson is not a story. What it means for us: a modern synthetic narrator is not ruled out by the evidence; the case against machine voices rested on old engines. Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131517301653 ; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0735633118802877

## 2. Narration voice and tone for science

### 2.1 The narrator's stance: first, second or third person

Ginns, Martin and Marsh's meta-analysis (1.3) is the main controlled evidence on person and address: first- and second-person address and a visible author improved retention and transfer in lessons (d = .30 and .54). Two further sources concern science video in particular, and both are observational.

**Welbourne and Grant, "Science communication on YouTube: factors that affect channel and video popularity" (2016).** [Study, content analysis; abstract] Dustin Welbourne and Will Grant of the Australian National University coded 390 videos from 39 science channels (Public Understanding of Science). Professionally made content was more plentiful, but user-generated content was significantly more popular. Channels with a consistent communicator (the same presenter or narrator) were more popular than channels without one, and user-generated videos delivered their content significantly faster. Limits: correlational; YouTube of the early 2010s; popularity measured by views and subscribers, not by understanding. What it means for us: a recognisable, consistent narrator (the same voice across films) goes with popularity, and so does getting to the content quickly. Sources: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963662515572068 ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272518559_Science_communication_on_YouTube_Factors_that_affect_channel_and_video_popularity

**Bunquin and Segre Cohen, "When scientists 'Tok: memetic communication and scientific information engagement in short video-based social media" (2026).** [Study, content analysis; abstract] Jon Benedik Bunquin (University of the Philippines Diliman) and Alex Segre Cohen (University of Oregon, Center for Science Communication Research) coded 1,025 science videos from 113 TikTok creators (Public Understanding of Science, 2026). Emotional and conversational language was widespread; fewer videos reused other people's media or told collective stories. According to a search summary of the abstract, imitation and humour positively predicted likes, shares, comments and views, and reappropriating existing media predicted less engagement. Limits: correlational; engagement, not understanding; the engagement result rests on a search summary. What it means for us: on the platform where these films are most likely to live, successful science videos speak conversationally and with feeling, and humour goes with engagement. Sources: https://doi.org/10.1177/09636625261469057 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42533308/

**INFERENCE on person for our films.** The person who built the simulator is a natural first-person narrator ("I set up a sea ...", "we watched ..."). First person has the personalization evidence behind it, and it makes honest attribution easy: "I think", "I don't know yet", "I only ran this once". Third person, the observer voice of the wildlife film (section 5), is equally possible; what the evidence favours is direct, conversational address, not a particular pronoun. Second person ("you'd expect ...") is the cheapest way to set up a surprise (2.5).

### 2.2 Humour

**Yeo, Anderson, Becker and Cacciatore, "Scientists as comedians: the effects of humor on perceptions of scientists and scientific messages" (2020).** [Study; abstract, results not obtained in detail] In Public Understanding of Science (29(4): 408-418), the authors embedded a video clip of a science comedian in an online survey and manipulated whether the audience in the clip laughed, to test how humour affects perceptions of scientists and whether comedy is seen as a valid source of scientific information. They note that humour had been recommended to scientists with little evidence behind it. I did not see the numerical findings. Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0963662520915359

**"Wit meets wisdom: the relationship between satire and anthropomorphic humor on scientists' likability and legitimacy" (Journal of Science Communication, 2025).** [Study; snippet] From the same research group at the University of Georgia. According to search summaries and the university's news release, satire and anthropomorphic humour increased a scientist's likability and the perceived legitimacy of the information, without undermining credibility. But the effect ran through mirth: humour helped only when people actually found it funny, and an attempt that did not amuse could do harm. The summaries add that science communication loses credibility if it carries too much sarcasm or negativity. Limits: rests on summaries; online experiments. Sources: https://jcom.sissa.it/article/pubid/JCOM_2401_2025_A04/ ; https://news.uga.edu/using-humor-in-communication-helps-scientist-connect/ ; https://jcom.sissa.it/news/28/

**"'That's some positive energy': how social media users respond to #funny science content" (Journal of Science Communication, 2024).** [Study; read] In an online experiment with 1,524 participants, social media posts about science used anthropomorphic humour, wordplay, both, or no humour. Every humour condition raised mirth over the control, the combination most. People who felt more mirth were more likely to leave a comment (445 of the 1,524 left a meaningful one), and "need for cognition" moderated both links. Limits: the outcome is engagement (commenting), not understanding, and the stimuli were static posts. Source: https://jcom.sissa.it/article/pubid/JCOM_2301_2024_A02/

**Sundararajan and Adesope, "Keep it coherent: a meta-analysis of the seductive details effect" (2020).** [Meta-analysis; abstract] In Educational Psychology Review, the authors pooled 68 studies of "seductive details": interesting but irrelevant additions to a lesson, such as fun facts or decorative anecdotes. Including them lowered learning (g = -0.33), a small to moderate harm, and the effect was moderated by delivery format, pacing, subject and other factors. Limits: lessons with tests; a seductive detail is by definition irrelevant to what is tested. Sources: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4 ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339511797_Keep_it_Coherent_A_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Seductive_Details_Effect

**What the humour evidence adds up to (INFERENCE).** A joke that is about the point, that makes the finding itself funny, has engagement evidence for it and nothing against it. A joke that is a detour, or a fun fact that is not the story, has a meta-analysis against it: it costs understanding. And a joke helps only if it lands, which the read-aloud test and an outside listener check better than the writer can. Muller's result (2.5) points the same way: adding interesting information to an explanation did not help, while stating the viewer's wrong expectation did. Ze Frank's True Facts (section 5) is the standing example of comic science narration done over real footage.

### 2.3 Story, wonder and hype

**Dahlstrom, "Using narratives and storytelling to communicate science with nonexpert audiences" (2014).** [Review; read] Michael Dahlstrom's paper in PNAS (111, supplement 4: 13614-13620), from the National Academy of Sciences' Sackler colloquium on the science of science communication, reviews the evidence on narrative against logical-scientific communication. He reports that narrative text was read about twice as fast and recalled about twice as well as expository text, "regardless of topic familiarity" (citing Graesser and colleagues), and that narratives offer increased recall, ease of comprehension and shorter reading times; nonexperts get most of their science from mass media, which already favour narrative. He also warns that narratives are "intrinsically persuasive": a story does not have to justify its claims, so claims inside it are accepted without the evidence being asked for, and are hard to counter. Narratives can perpetuate misinformation, and a non-representative example can steer an audience to a conclusion. He asks communicators to decide whether their aim is comprehension or persuasion, and how much accuracy the story needs: whether it must be realistic, and whether its case is representative. Limits: a review; the doubling figures come from reading studies. What it means for us: telling an experiment as a story (a question, what we expected, what happened, what it means) is well supported. The accuracy duty falls on whether the case shown is representative (one dramatic creature, or what the population did), and on not letting the story's momentum carry a claim the data do not make. Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4183170/ ; https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1320645111

**Sumner and colleagues, "Exaggerations and caveats in press releases and health-related science news" (2016).** [Study; abstract] In PLOS ONE, the Cardiff University group analysed 534 press releases on biomedical and health research issued by leading peer-reviewed journals, the 534 papers behind them, and 582 news stories. Exaggerations and caveats in the news mostly originated in the press release, and they found no evidence that exaggeration increased, or caveats decreased, the chance that a release was covered. Limits: biomedical press releases; news uptake, not audience response. What it means for us: the fear that a caveat makes a story less attractive is not borne out where it has been measured, and hype did not buy coverage. Sources: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0168217 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27978540/

**Attenborough on truthfulness (Open University, Earth in Vision).** [Practitioner; read, through the fetch tool's paraphrase] In an interview for the Open University's Earth in Vision project, Attenborough says films must be truthful and must not distort things "in the name of sensationalism", and describes speaking commentary that he "thought spoke the truth about the biology of the polar bear" even where the filming had required compromises. Source: https://www.open.edu/openlearn/nature-environment/the-environment/creative-climate/earth-vision/sir-david-attenborough-earth-vision

**Kurzgesagt on research and fact-checking.** [Practitioner; snippet, their own post refused the fetch] The Munich animation studio behind one of the largest science channels describes its process on Medium: deep reading of books and primary papers; consultation with several experts; about a dozen script drafts to cut and polish; and fact-checkers who take the plain script statement by statement, check that each fact is current and generally accepted, recalculate back-of-the-envelope numbers, and judge whether a concept has been simplified so far as to mislead. Simplifications are documented in a sources document published with every video, and fact-checking and sourcing take around 100 hours a video. What it means for us: the practice that goes with a trusted science channel is to check every sentence of the script against the record, and to write down each simplification the narration makes. Source: https://medium.com/@Kurzgesagt/how-research-and-factchecking-work-at-kurzgesagt-f5b239188255

### 2.4 Accurate without hedging every line

**Jensen, "Scientific uncertainty in news coverage of cancer research: effects of hedging on scientists' and journalists' credibility" (2008).** [Study; abstract] Jakob Jensen (Human Communication Research 34(3)) ran a multiple-message experiment with 601 participants and five news stories about cancer research. Scientists and journalists were both seen as more trustworthy when the story was hedged (reported the study's limitations), and particularly when the hedge was attributed to the scientists who did the research. The paper notes that news reports of research are rarely hedged. Limits: health news in print. Sources: https://academic.oup.com/hcr/article-abstract/34/3/347/4210781 ; author's copy https://jakobdjensen.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/2008_Jensen_Human-Com-Research.pdf

**What this adds up to.** Taken with van der Bles and colleagues (1.6) and Sumner and colleagues (2.3): stating the limits of a finding does not cost trust, and when the scientist states them it gains trust. That is EVIDENCE. None of these studies tested how many hedges a text can carry, or hedging spread thin across every verb ("may", "might", "could potentially", "appears to"). INFERENCE: the one-idea-per-sentence limit argues for stating each limit once, plainly, where it applies, in the narrator's own voice ("we only ran this once, so it could be chance"), and then telling the rest of the story plainly. A hedge on every verb spends the listener's small store on qualifiers and blurs which claims are firm and which are not.

### 2.5 Setting up the wrong answer first

**Muller, Bewes, Sharma and Reimann, "Saying the wrong thing: improving learning with multimedia by including misconceptions" (2008).** [Study; abstract] Derek Muller, later the creator of the Veritasium channel, did this work for his PhD at the University of Sydney (Journal of Computer Assisted Learning 24(2): 144-155). He randomly assigned 364 first-year physics students to one of four online videos on Newton's first and second laws: an Exposition, a concise lecture-style explanation; an Extended Exposition, the same with extra interesting information; a Refutation, the Exposition with common misconceptions stated and then refuted; and a Dialogue, a student and a tutor discussing the same material as the Refutation. Students answered conceptual-inventory questions before and after. The Refutation and the Dialogue produced the largest gains, with effect sizes of 0.79 and 0.83 against the Exposition; the extra interesting information produced no such gain. Students with low prior knowledge gained most, and those with high prior knowledge were not held back. A companion paper (Muller, Sharma and Reimann 2008, Science Education 92(2): 278-296) argues that the misconception-based videos raised mental effort, and that this helped. Limits: physics misconceptions, students, a lesson rather than a story. What it means for us: stating what a viewer would expect ("you'd think the fastest swimmers would win") before showing what the simulation did is backed by an unusually large effect, and it is also how a surprise is built. Adding colourful extra facts is not. Sources: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2729.2007.00248.x ; https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Saying-the-wrong-thing:-improving-learning-with-by-Muller-Bewes/e8d17d8fa764581b5c3dbcccacbcf8c3072bcec3 ; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sce.20244 ; Veritasium's list of the publications: https://www.veritasium.com/publications

### 2.6 Purpose and intention in stories about evolution

This matters more for our films than for most science video. They are about evolution, and the easiest words for what evolving populations do ("learned to", "tried to", "wanted", "in order to") are the words that carry the commonest misconceptions.

**Kelemen and colleagues on "promiscuous teleology".** [Studies; through a review] Deborah Kelemen's work shows that people readily explain nature by purpose. Kelemen and Rosset (2009) found that undergraduates endorsed statements such as "finches diversified in order to survive" and "fungi grow in forests to help decomposition", and more so under time pressure. Kelemen and colleagues (2013) found that even professional physical scientists fell back on purpose-based explanations under cognitive load. Reviews in evolution education call the tendency persistent across development, and note that textbooks and experts often use teleological and anthropomorphic language themselves. Limits: I saw these through a review, not the papers. Sources: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01839/full ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/279235531_Teleological_minds_How_natural_intuitions_about_agency_and_purpose_influence_learning_about_evolution

**Nielson, Pitt, Fux, de Nesnera, Betz, Leffers, Tanner and Coley, "Spontaneous anthropocentric language use in university students' explanations of biological concepts varies by topic and predicts misconception agreement" (2025).** [Study; abstract] In CBE-Life Sciences Education (24(1)), 807 undergraduates at two US universities answered open questions on five core biology concepts, evolution among them. Most used some intuitive, "construal-consistent" language; how much varied by concept; and students who used it agreed more strongly with misconception statements, a relationship driven by anthropocentric language (language centred on humans). Limits: correlational, and about students' own words, not the effect of hearing such words. Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39808720/ ; https://www.lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.24-07-0198

**Kampourakis and others on the design stance.** [Review; abstract] A line of argument in Evolution: Education and Outreach holds that the problem in students' "teleological misconceptions" is not talk of function or purpose as such, but the underlying design stance: the idea that traits exist because something intended or needed them. Sources: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12052-019-0116-z ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12052-020-00118-8

**Sealey and Oakley, "Anthropomorphic grammar? Some linguistic patterns in the wildlife documentary series Life" (2013).** [Study, corpus analysis; abstract] Alison Sealey and Lucy Oakley (Text and Talk 33(3)) analysed a corpus of transcripts of the BBC series Life, narrated by Attenborough. Looking past obvious word choices, they examined three grammatical features, pronouns, the connective "so", and the "to"-infinitive, and showed that separately and together these represent animals' perspectives and convey intention and evaluation in subtle ways. What it means for us: the grammar carries the intention even when no verb of wanting is used. "So it can reach the food" and "it swims up to feed" both assign a purpose. Sources: https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/text-2013-0017/html?lang=en ; https://www.academia.edu/3770815/Anthropomorphic_grammar_Some_linguistic_patterns_in_the_wildlife_documentary_series_Life ; a companion paper on the same corpus: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259550108_Why_did_the_Canada_goose_cross_the_sea_Accounting_for_the_behaviour_of_wildlife_in_the_documentary_series_Life

**Dingwall and Aldridge, "Television wildlife programming as a source of popular scientific information: a case study of evolution" (2006).** [Study; abstract] In Public Understanding of Science (15(2): 131-152), Robert Dingwall and Meryl Aldridge examined how television wildlife programmes represent evolution, and concluded that the narrative and commercial imperatives of "blue-chip" productions shape how the science is represented. Source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228348050_Television_Wildlife_Programming_as_a_Source_of_Popular_Scientific_Information_A_Case_Study_of_Evolution

**Geerdts, Van de Walle and LoBue, "Learning about real animals from anthropomorphic media" (2016).** [Study; abstract] In Imagination, Cognition and Personality (36(1): 5-26), an experiment with children found that anthropomorphic language and pictures in storybooks did not interfere with factual learning about real animals. The same group's review notes that the studies finding no harm used milder anthropomorphism, such as anthropomorphic language alone with realistic images. What it means for us: anthropomorphic language does not always harm the learning of facts; the concern is specific to explanations of why traits change, where it maps onto the design stance. Sources: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0276236615611798 ; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/cdep.12153

**What this adds up to (INFERENCE, on evidence that is partly correlational).** In an evolution story, say what happened to populations and lineages, not what creatures wanted or tried: "bodies with longer fins left more young", "within a few hundred generations most of them had ...", "the ones that did this survived, and the others did not". Avoid "evolved to", "learned to", "in order to", "so that", "needed", "decided" and "figured out" when explaining a change across generations. A single creature's behaviour, which in our simulator comes from an evolved brain, can be told in plain action verbs ("it turns toward the food"), but never in verbs of intention about its lineage's future. Anthropomorphism is safe as an obvious joke (the humour studies used it successfully), not as the explanation.

## 3. How narration works with the picture

### 3.1 Words should be about what is on screen

A consistent line of television news research finds that people remember more when the words and the pictures are about the same thing, and that pictures unrelated to the words do little good.

**Reese, "Visual-verbal redundancy effects on television news learning" (1984).** [Study; citation only] Stephen Reese's study in the Journal of Broadcasting (28(1): 79-87) is the early experiment in this line. I saw only the citation. Source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254298576_Visual-verbal_redundancy_effects_on_television_news_learning

**Drew and Grimes, "Audio-visual redundancy and TV news recall" (1987).** [Study; abstract] In Communication Research (14(4): 452-461), college students watched voice-over news stories whose pictures matched the narration to different degrees. In the high-redundancy condition they recalled more of what was said and understood the stories better. Sources: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/009365087014004005 ; https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ359196

**Brosius, Donsbach and Birk, "How do text-picture relations affect the informational effectiveness of television newscasts?" (1996).** [Study; abstract, through a later paper's summary] In the Journal of Broadcasting and Electronic Media (40(2): 180-195), stories were shown with video that illustrated the audio, video mildly related to it, video unrelated to it, or no video. Free and cued recall were best when the video illustrated the audio. The other three conditions did not differ significantly, though the means ran from mildly related, to unrelated, to no video. What it means for us: generic footage under specific narration ("some creatures swimming" while the words tell a lineage's history) does little more than no footage at all. Sources: https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/16597531/6.pdf (a later paper that summarises it) ; https://newslab.org/tv-news-research-bibliography/

**Lang, "Defining audio/video redundancy from a limited-capacity information processing perspective" (1995).** [Study/theory; citation] Annie Lang's paper in Communication Research frames redundancy in terms of limited processing capacity: when sound and picture carry related information, the load of processing both falls. Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/009365095022001004

**Fox, "A signal detection analysis of audio/video redundancy effects in television news video" (2004).** [Study; abstract] Julia Fox of Indiana University (Communication Research 31(5)) used signal detection methods and found that viewers discriminated information from stories with redundant visuals better than from stories with dissonant visuals. The redundancy effect reflected memory strength, not a shift in how willing people were to say they recognised something. Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0093650204267931

Son, Reese and Davie (1987), described in 1.5, found the same direction, and found that matching beat recapping.

**Ginns, "Integrating information: a meta-analysis of the spatial contiguity and temporal contiguity effects" (2006).** [Meta-analysis; abstract, through secondary summaries] Paul Ginns (Learning and Instruction) pooled 13 experiments on temporal contiguity and found an average effect of d = 0.87 in favour of presenting narration at the same time as the picture it explains, rather than before or after it. The same paper pooled 37 comparisons on spatial contiguity (placing text next to what it describes). Limits: instructional animations. What it means for us: a sentence about a shot must be heard while that shot is on screen, not over the shot before or after it. This is one of the strongest effects in the whole literature. Sources: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/248498142_Integrating_information_A_meta-analysis_of_the_spatial_contiguity_and_temporal_contiguity_effects ; Mayer's overview of the multimedia principles: https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf

### 3.2 But not a description of what the viewer can see

**Tompkins, "To tell stronger stories, don't narrate the video. Explain it." (Poynter, 2021).** [Practitioner; read] Al Tompkins, a longtime Poynter Institute faculty member for broadcast and online journalism whose honours include a national Emmy and a Peabody, argues that "Words and pictures should not compete." Narrating what viewers already see gives them nothing. His example of what not to write: "CNN reporter Boris Sanchez stands in a parking lot about to go on the air. There is debris in the background." Instead, the words should add what the pictures cannot: that the anchor has covered hundreds of emergencies and has to set sentiment aside and focus on the facts; or why a network crew needs so much gear for a live shot when a local journalist might be out there alone with a camera, a tripod and a phone. His textbook Aim for the Heart develops the same teaching. Sources: https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2021/to-tell-stronger-stories-dont-narrate-the-video-explain-it/ ; https://aimfortheheart.com/handouts/

**Attenborough on commentary (BAFTA interview).** [Practitioner; snippet, not verified] A search summary of BAFTA's transcript of a "Natural History with Sir David Attenborough" event quotes him saying that as a producer in the 1950s the aim was to "have no commentary at all, what you wanted to do was the pictures to tell the story because it could", and that you would "restrict your words to the bits of information that were necessary to understand the pictures". It also describes him writing commentary at the screen and being surprised by the action before he had written anything. The BAFTA page refused the fetch, and an exact-phrase search did not find the quotation elsewhere, so the wording is unverified; its sense agrees with everything else known of his method. Source: https://bafta.org/media-centre/transcripts/natural-history-with-sir-david-attenborough

**Reconciling the two (INFERENCE, well grounded).** In the research, "redundancy" means topical correspondence: words and picture about the same event. It does not mean the words restating what is visible. The news studies and Tompkins therefore agree: talk about what is on screen at that moment, and say what the eye cannot get from it. For our footage, the things the picture cannot show are exactly the story: what the creature is (a simulated body run by an evolved brain), how many generations separate two shots, which lineage it belongs to, what rule was changed, what the numbers did, what we expected, and what it means. "Here a creature swims to the left" adds nothing. "This one's great-grandparents could not swim at all" adds what the picture cannot.

### 3.3 Timing the words to the cut

**Attenborough's working method.** [Commentary; snippet] A Cinemablend article reports that Attenborough writes to the edited picture on a VHS tape, moving backwards and forwards until the words fit, taking up to a week to get the commentary of a one-hour programme right and then recording it in about two hours. In earlier series he wrote to every shot himself; now producers draft a script to suit his style. The piece notes that the script has to be written to the facts of the footage, because the footage cannot be changed to fit a script. The Ringer (2026) describes his use of silence like punctuation, with long pauses as he watches the cut (snippet). Sources: https://www.cinemablend.com/television/1600210/david-attenborough-still-uses-vhs-to-script-planet-earth ; https://www.theringer.com/2026/05/04/tv/david-attenborough-voice-nature-earth-100-years

**Documentary practitioners on writing to picture.** [Practitioner; snippet] A documentary editing site advises that if a 20-second bridge needs narration, you write 15 seconds of copy, because stretching words to fill time is easier than cutting them; that pauses highlight dramatic moments; and that narration should reinforce the video, not compete with it. A voice-over trainer (Peter Baker) says there are usually gaps where the narrator is silent and the pictures do the talking, and recommends leaving a couple of seconds between sentences for flexibility in the edit. Videomaker's guide advises using words only when you have to and otherwise letting the visuals carry the message, and reading the script aloud to hear whether it sounds engaging or like a textbook. Sources: https://newdocediting.com/ten-tips-for-writing-documentary-narration/ ; https://medium.com/@voiceovermasterclass/10-tips-to-nail-that-tv-documentary-narration-6e6b5251a1c0 ; https://www.videomaker.com/article/f5/7356-writing-effective-narration/ ; https://newdocediting.com/when-to-use-documentary-narration/

**The turn against wall-to-wall narration.** [Commentary; snippet] Kate Taylor's column in the Globe and Mail (2015) on the Hot Docs festival describes how the documentary world came to treat the heavily narrated "voice of God" as poor storytelling and false authority. Source: https://www.theglobeandmail.com/arts/film/kate-taylor-why-the-narrating-voice-of-god-has-disappeared-at-hot-docs/article24106026/

**What this means for us.** Temporal contiguity (EVIDENCE) says a sentence belongs on its shot. Practice says to write to the cut, under-fill each gap (about three quarters of the available time), and leave the key visual moments without words. Rodero (1.7) warns that slow and sparse also loses attention, so a silence has to be earned by something worth watching (INFERENCE). On short-form platforms a silent opening is expensive (3.5).

### 3.4 Words per minute and a word budget

The figures bearing on pace come from four directions. Rodero's radio study found 170 to 190 Spanish words a minute best for recognition, without pictures (1.7). The voice-over trade narrates documentaries at around 130 to 140 English words a minute (1.7). Subtitle standards cap reading at 160 to 180 words a minute (BBC), 130 to 160 (DCMP, by the viewer's reading level) or 20 characters a second for adults (Netflix) (4.1). And comprehension of heard speech holds up far above all of these (Foulke and Sticht; Murphy and colleagues).

INFERENCE: with subtitles that follow the narration word for word, a narration pace of about 140 to 150 words a minute keeps the subtitles inside the BBC and DCMP ranges and leaves attention for the picture. Twenty characters a second is roughly 200 words a minute at about six characters a word, space included (my arithmetic), so the Netflix ceiling is looser. A budget follows: at 140 words a minute, a 60-second film with a few seconds of picture at each end and a couple of held moments carries roughly 100 to 130 spoken words. Each second of narration holds about two and a third words.

### 3.5 What the platforms say about short-form video

**Meta for Business, "Capture attention with updated features for video ads".** [Platform data; read] Facebook's advertiser page (undated on the page; the features it announces date from about 2016) reports that "65% of people who watch the first three seconds of a video will watch for at least ten seconds" and "45% continue watching for thirty seconds"; that "up to 47% of the value in a video campaign was delivered in the first three seconds, while up to 74% of the value was delivered in the first ten"; that in one Facebook study "41% of videos were basically meaningless without sound"; and that "Captioned video ads increase video view time by an average of 12%". Limits: advertisements, Facebook's news feed of the mid-2010s, undisclosed method, an interested publisher. Source: https://www.facebook.com/business/news/updated-features-for-video-ads

**TikTok, creative best practices ("Creative Codes").** [Platform data; read] TikTok's business blog states that "90% of ad recall impact is captured within the first six seconds", that for "88% of TikTok users, sound is vital to the TikTok experience", and that "creative attributes that get people to read increase view time, drive recall, and make ads more likable"; it recommends a three-part structure of hook, body and close. A TikTok creative-tips document widely quoted by agencies says that over 63% of the videos with the highest click-through rates highlight their key message or product within the first three seconds; I could not read the document itself, so that figure is second-hand. Limits: advertisements, undated research from about 2020 to 2022, an interested publisher. Sources: https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en/blog/creative-best-practices-top-performing-ads ; https://ads.tiktok.com/business/library/Auction_Ads_Creative_Tips.pdf

**TikTok and Kantar on sound (2021).** [Platform data; read] TikTok's blog post of 8 June 2021 reports a Kantar study in which "88% of TikTok users said that sound is essential to the TikTok experience", 73% said they would "stop and look" at ads on TikTok with audio, and users experienced sound on TikTok as fun at a 66% higher rate than on other platforms. The page gives no sample or method. Source: https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en/blog/kantar-report-how-brands-are-making-noise-and-driving-impact-with-sound-on-tiktok

**Verizon Media and Publicis Media (2019).** [Platform data; snippet] An online survey of 5,616 US adults aged 18 to 54, in April 2019, found that 69% watch video with the sound off in public places and 80% say they are more likely to watch a whole video when captions are available. Limits: self-report, commissioned by media companies. Sources: https://www.forbes.com/sites/tjmccue/2019/07/31/verizon-media-says-69-percent-of-consumers-watching-video-with-sound-off/ ; https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/verizon-media-and-publicis-media-find-viewers-want-captions/

**Szarkowska and colleagues, "Watching subtitled videos with the sound off affects viewers' comprehension, cognitive load, immersion, enjoyment, and gaze patterns" (2024).** [Study; read] In PLOS ONE, 161 viewers (43 British and 53 Australian native speakers of English, and 65 Polish speakers of English as a second language; mean age about 25) watched English-subtitled videos (subtitles at 16.85 characters a second on average) with the sound on or off, with eye tracking. Comprehension was a little lower without sound (77.7% against 81.3%, a small effect). Cognitive load (difficulty, effort, frustration) was much higher without sound (large effects), and immersion and enjoyment were lower. Without sound, viewers fixated the subtitles longer, read them longer and skipped fewer words. What it means for us: a sound-off viewer can follow a well-subtitled film, but works harder and enjoys it less; the subtitles must be written to be read, and the film should not depend on anything that is only heard. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11458047/

**Short-form science video across platforms (AGU Fall Meeting 2023).** [Conference abstract; snippet] An abstract by geoscience communicators on TikTok, Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts reports that for videos cross-posted to several platforms, median views were three times higher on TikTok than on Reels and 25 times higher than on Shorts. Its practitioner survey describes the hook as the opening two to five seconds, carried by a question, a surprising fact or a relatable problem. Source: https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023AGUFMED11D0793Z/abstract

**What this means for us (PLATFORM plus INFERENCE).** The first spoken sentence, and the first frame's text, must say what the film is about within about three seconds. The platform data concern advertisements, but the attention curve they describe applies to any video in a feed. The film has to work twice: with sound, as a narrated story (TikTok's audience says sound is essential), and without, from subtitles and on-screen text (much feed viewing elsewhere is silent). Welbourne and Grant's finding that faster delivery went with popularity (2.1) points the same way.

## 4. Subtitles and on-screen text

### 4.1 The standards

**Netflix, English (USA) Timed Text Style Guide.** [Guideline; read] The streaming service's delivery standard for English subtitles allows 42 characters per line and at most two lines ("Text should usually be kept to one line, unless it exceeds the character limitation"), and a reading speed of up to 20 characters per second for adult programmes and 17 for children's. When a subtitle breaks onto two lines, the break goes after punctuation, before conjunctions and before prepositions. It must not separate an article from its noun, an adjective from its noun, a first name from a last name, a subject from its verb, a preposition from its verb, or a verb from its auxiliary or its negation. A "bottom-heavy pyramid" shape is preferred, with no single word on the top line. Netflix's separate timing guidelines (seen through search) set a minimum of five sixths of a second and a maximum of seven seconds per subtitle, a minimum gap of two frames between subtitles, and rules for snapping a subtitle's in and out times to a shot change. Sources: https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide ; https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/360051554394-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-Subtitle-Timing-Guidelines ; https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-General-Requirements

**BBC Subtitle Guidelines.** [Guideline; read through a secondary summary, the BBC site refused the fetch] The BBC recommends a subtitle speed of 160 to 180 words a minute (0.33 to 0.375 seconds per word); 37 characters per line for Teletext, and online a line no wider than 68% of a 16:9 frame (90% of a 4:3 frame); a maximum of two lines; and breaks "at logical points", ideally at a full stop, a comma or a dash. When a good line break cannot be combined with well-edited text or good timing, "the latter should take precedence". Sources: https://www.bbc.co.uk/accessibility/forproducts/guides/subtitles/ ; summary https://www.clevercast.com/bbc-subtitling-guidelines/ ; https://broadcastwriter.com/2024/12/12/bbc-subtitle-style-guide-2024/

**DCMP Captioning Key.** [Guideline; read] The Described and Captioned Media Program sets 32 characters per line and one or two lines per caption, and presentation rates for educational media of no more than 130 words a minute at lower levels, 140 at middle levels and 160 at upper levels. DCMP supports editing captions to meet the rate, "based on research results" and its half-century of experience, and describes the debate: organisations of deaf and hard-of-hearing people favour verbatim captions as a matter of equal access, while parents and teachers favour editing because verbatim rates can be impossible to follow. Editing is "often essential to ensure that students have time to read the captions, integrate the captions and picture, and internalize and comprehend the message", and when it is done, "each caption should maintain the meaning, content, and essential vocabulary of the original narration". Quotations, well-known speakers, published works and song lyrics stay verbatim. Sources: https://dcmp.org/learn/601-captioning-key---presentation-rate ; https://dcmp.org/learn/5-captioning-guidelines-for-the-dcmp ; https://dcmp.org/learn/597-captioning-key---text

### 4.2 Evidence on how subtitles are read

**Szarkowska and Gerber-Moron, "Viewers can keep up with fast subtitles: evidence from eye movements" (2018).** [Study; abstract] In PLOS ONE, 74 English, Polish and Spanish viewers watched films subtitled at 12, 16 and 20 characters a second, with eye tracking. Most viewers read the subtitles and followed the images well, even at the fastest speed. The study also examined whether coping depended on familiarity with subtitling and on knowing the soundtrack's language. Limits: films with their soundtrack on; subtitles translating that soundtrack. Sources: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0199331 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6007935/

**Liao, Yu, Kruger and Reichle (2021).** [Study; snippet, through a replication's summary] Thirty-one native Australian English speakers watched videos without sound, with English subtitles at 12, 20 and 28 characters a second, some over moving images and some on a black screen, with eye tracking and eight comprehension questions per video. At 28 characters a second comprehension fell, fixations were fewer and shorter, and the gaze moved less between the subtitles and the picture. The presence of video improved comprehension at every speed. The usual reading effects (long and rare words drawing more attention) weakened at high speed. A replication and extension followed in the Journal of Audiovisual Translation, and Kruger, Wisniewska and Liao (2022, Applied Psycholinguistics, "Why subtitle speed matters: evidence from word skipping and rereading") examined skipping and rereading at speed (title only). Sources: https://jatjournal.org/index.php/jat/article/view/283 ; https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/188188/1/Szarkowska_final+locked.pdf ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/abs/why-subtitle-speed-matters-evidence-from-word-skipping-and-rereading/A7DB0CBF5910353143738CF9C1721317 ; https://ausit.org/blog/fast-subtitles-cause-superficial-reading-evidence-from-eye-movements/

**Gerber-Moron, Szarkowska and Woll, "The impact of text segmentation on subtitle reading" (2018).** [Study; abstract] In the Journal of Eye Movement Research (11(4)), viewers (27 English, 21 Polish and 26 Spanish native speakers in the first experiment) watched two-line subtitles broken either at syntactic boundaries or across them, with an eye tracker. All three measures of cognitive load were consistently higher when lines were not broken at syntactic units. Earlier research on the question had been inconclusive, and professionals had assumed the answer without evidence. A companion paper studied viewers' preferences for line breaks. Sources: https://bop.unibe.ch/JEMR/article/view/4267 ; https://www.mdpi.com/1995-8692/11/4/27 ; https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10051115/

**Gernsbacher, "Video captions benefit everyone" (2015).** [Review; abstract] Morton Ann Gernsbacher of the University of Wisconsin-Madison (Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences 2(1): 195-202) reviewed more than 100 empirical studies and concluded that same-language captions improve comprehension of, attention to and memory for video. The benefit holds for viewers watching in a second language, for children and adults learning to read, for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, and also for literate hearing adults. Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5214590/ ; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/2372732215602130

Szarkowska and colleagues (2024), in 3.5, add that sound-off viewers read subtitles for longer and skip fewer words.

### 4.3 The redundancy tension: captions against the multimedia research

The multimedia-learning literature has a well-known "redundancy principle": do not also put the narration on screen as text. It sits uneasily with the caption evidence above, and the details resolve most of the conflict.

**Kalyuga, Chandler and Sweller, "When redundant on-screen text in multimedia technical instruction can interfere with learning" (2004).** [Study; abstract] In Human Factors (46(3)), technical instruction presented in spoken form alone beat the same instruction spoken and also shown as identical on-screen text. The explanation offered is the working-memory cost of reconciling identical words arriving by eye and by ear. Source: https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.3.567.50405

**Adesope and Nesbit, "Verbal redundancy in multimedia learning environments: a meta-analysis" (2012).** [Meta-analysis; abstract] Olusola Adesope and John Nesbit (Journal of Educational Psychology) pooled 57 studies of text presented together with the same words spoken. Spoken-plus-written did not differ from written-only, but beat spoken-only overall. The advantage over spoken-only depended on the learner and the material: it held for learners with low prior knowledge, for system-paced material (such as video, where the viewer does not control the pace), and for material without pictures. And presentations showing only key terms extracted from the narration did better than verbatim text, accounting for much of the advantage of spoken-plus-written over spoken-only. Sources: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232469670_Verbal_Redundancy_in_Multimedia_Learning_Environments_A_Meta-Analysis ; https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Verbal-redundancy-in-multimedia-learning-A-Adesope-Nesbit/f3cf19bfea0d7d51f6914cb61a7f89f49d7666dc

**Mayer and Johnson, "Revising the redundancy principle in multimedia learning" (2008).** [Study; abstract] In the Journal of Educational Psychology (100(2): 380-386), students watched narrated slide lessons on how lightning forms (16 slides) or how car brakes work (8 slides), each slide on screen for 8 to 10 seconds with a diagram and one or two sentences of narration in a female voice. For one group each slide also carried two or three printed words, identical to words in the narration, conveying the main event and placed next to the relevant part of the diagram. That group remembered more (d = 0.47 and 0.70 in the two experiments) but did no better on transfer. The authors explain that short text placed at the diagram guides attention without the extra processing that full redundant text causes. Sources: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ796353 ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232540768_Revising_the_Redundancy_Principle_in_Multimedia_Learning

**Resolving it for our films (INFERENCE, grounded in the above).** Subtitles and graphics do two different jobs. Subtitles are the sound-off and accessibility channel, and on social platforms a large share of viewers need them (3.5). They should follow the narration word for word, or nearly, because a viewer with the sound on who also reads them will notice any mismatch, and because the DCMP's editing rule keeps the meaning and the essential vocabulary in any case. The redundancy research says nothing against subtitles for a sound-off viewer, who has no narration for them to be redundant with. Graphics and on-screen labels are a different matter: the evidence favours a few words (the key term or the number) placed at the thing they name and timed with the sentence that says it, never a sentence repeating the narration. The meta-analysis's finding that key terms beat verbatim text is the strongest guide here.

**On vertical video (INFERENCE).** The line lengths above were set for 16:9 television and film. On a 9:16 phone frame, 42 characters at a readable size may not fit on one line; shorter lines, one line at a time where possible, with the same break rules, are the natural adaptation. I found no study of subtitle line length on vertical video.

**Word-by-word animated captions.** The word-by-word "karaoke" captions common on TikTok (a short phrase on screen, each word highlighted as it is spoken) have no controlled study behind them that I found. A 2024 ACM paper on keyword highlights and time-synchronised captions for language learners is titled "Useful but distracting" (abstract seen only). Claims that animated captions raise watch time come from caption-tool vendors, and are marketing, not evidence. Sources: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3701571.3701574 ; https://kreateflo.com/blog/which-animated-caption-styles-actually-increase-video-watch-time ; https://voicecreator.pro/blog/word-by-word-animated-captions

### 4.4 How subtitles differ from the narration

The narration is written for the ear and paced by the voice; the subtitles are the same words cut into readable pieces and paced by the reader. Several differences follow from the sources. Subtitles are broken at phrase boundaries, never in the middle of a phrase (Netflix; Gerber-Moron and colleagues). Each stays on screen between five sixths of a second and seven seconds (Netflix), at no more than 17 to 20 characters a second (Netflix) or 160 to 180 words a minute (BBC), with its timing snapped to shot changes (Netflix). Where the narration runs too fast for its subtitles, the fix belongs in the script (fewer words), because editing the subtitles away from the narration creates a mismatch (INFERENCE). Numbers can be shown as digits in subtitles and in on-screen text even when they are spoken as words (INFERENCE from common practice; I did not verify a guideline for it).

## 5. Narration held up as excellent, and what its writing does

**David Attenborough and the BBC Natural History Unit** (Life on Earth, The Living Planet, Planet Earth, The Blue Planet, Life; Our Planet for Netflix). [Commentary and practitioner; mixed] Attenborough's narration is the reference point for nature film. William Ratcliff went as far as proposing an "Attenborough style" of scientific talk in Trends in Molecular Medicine (2023; abstract seen): the speaker as the narrator of an enthralling story, the visuals streamlined, so that the audience becomes immersed in the narrative and the images and forgets the speaker is there. What the writing does, from the sources in 3.2 and 3.3: it is written to the finished cut; it limits itself to what is needed to understand the pictures; it leaves long silences; and it says what the picture cannot show (why, how rare, what happens next). The caution is Sealey and Oakley's (2.6): its grammar routinely gives animals intentions, which for our evolution stories is exactly what to avoid. Sources: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37479552 ; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1471491423000928 ; also the BAFTA, Cinemablend, Ringer and Open University sources above; Netflix's article with Our Planet II filmmakers on his voice (not read): https://www.netflix.com/tudum/articles/working-with-david-attenborough-our-planet-2

**Carl Sagan, Cosmos (1980) and "Pale Blue Dot" (1994).** [Institutional record and commentary; snippet] The Library of Congress added Sagan's 1994 recording of "Pale Blue Dot" to the US National Recording Registry in 2023. Commentators praise how he wove difficult, abstract concepts into narrative, with honesty and humility about humanity's place, balancing the practical and the wondrous. INFERENCE from the text itself: the passage anchors an unimaginable scale to one picture everyone is looking at (a single pixel), speaks as "we" and "us", and moves from the fact to what it means. Sources: https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/pale-blue-dot.pdf ; https://ekostories.com/2012/08/11/sagan-pale-blue-dot/ ; https://www.space.com/15994-carl-sagan.html

**KQED Deep Look.** [Institutional; read] A short-form series (about three to five minutes an episode) from KQED Science with PBS, built on macro photography of small creatures; its first press release called the episodes "science tapas". It has won Northern California Emmys (2017, 2018 and 2019 among others), a Jackson Hole Science Media Award for best short-form series (2018), a Webby People's Voice award (2017) and a Jackson Hole award for best limited series (2015); 70% of its viewers are aged 18 to 34, "much younger than the traditional PBS primetime viewer". Its writer and host says it "asks how and why things happen at a very tiny scale". Search summaries describe its writing as humorous and pithy. What its writing does: one question per episode, answered with the footage. Sources: https://www.kqed.org/pressroom/10803/deeplook1million ; https://www.kqed.org/pressroom/7306/deep-look ; https://www.kqed.org/pressroom/11248/kqed-wins-two-northern-california-emmy-awards

**Ze Frank, True Facts.** [Commentary; snippet] Short parody nature documentaries, narrated by Ze Frank over researchers' footage, with comic phrasing, odd comparisons and tangents, yet "reasonably accurate"; some are made with biologists, and Frank has said he tries to "let the footage drive this conversation" rather than impose a human story on the animals. What its writing does: the joke is about the real biology, so the humour carries the fact (2.2). Sources: https://uscatholic.org/articles/202108/ze-frank-true-facts-are-full-of-wit-and-wonder/ ; https://studybreaks.com/tvfilm/ze-frank/ ; https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/WebVideo/TrueFacts ; https://www.fastcompany.com/1682681/learn-true-facts-about-sloths-moles-and-making-a-youtube-hit-ze-frank-reinvents-the-nature-s (refused the fetch)

**Kurzgesagt ("In a Nutshell").** [Practitioner; snippet] Animated science explainers from one of the most subscribed science channels, narrated in a first-person plural "we" with understatement and humour, written through about a dozen drafts and fact-checked line by line, with a published sources document that records the simplifications (2.3). Sources: https://medium.com/@Kurzgesagt/how-research-and-factchecking-work-at-kurzgesagt-f5b239188255 ; https://kurzgesagt.org/what-we-do?visit=videos

**Veritasium (Derek Muller).** [Study and practitioner] A channel whose style grew out of its creator's research (2.5): open with what the viewer thinks will happen, show that it does not, then explain. In his experiments this raised learning by an effect size of about 0.8. Source: https://www.veritasium.com/publications

**Primer (Justin Helps).** [Commentary; read] The closest example to our films: animated simulations of blob creatures whose traits evolve under natural selection, created and narrated in the first person by the maker. The 2019 video "Simulating Natural Selection" was widely shared (kottke.org and FlowingData among others), and kottke described it as showing "how different environments cause groups to tend towards certain traits (size, speed, sensing ability) based on food availability, with random mutation and reproduction in the mix as well", adding: "Seeing the populations' traits change in realtime as the generations pass is a powerful way to make sense of a complex concept." What its writing does (INFERENCE, from the descriptions): the narration explains the rule, the picture shows the population changing, and the numbers are shown as graphs rather than spoken. Sources: https://kottke.org/19/07/simulating-natural-selection ; https://flowingdata.com/2019/07/31/natural-selection-simulation/ ; https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0ZGbIKd0XrM ; https://youtube.fandom.com/wiki/Primer

## 6. Common faults that lose or confuse listeners

Each fault below is named with the source that names it, or the evidence against it, and its label.

**Narrating what is visible.** "Here a creature swims to the left" competes with the picture and adds nothing; the viewer can see it. Tompkins and Attenborough (3.2). PRACTICE.

**Words about something other than what is on screen, or generic footage under specific narration.** Recall falls to the level of no pictures at all. Drew and Grimes, Brosius and colleagues, Fox (3.1). EVIDENCE.

**Words out of step with the shot they describe.** A sentence heard over the shot before or after the one it is about loses most of the benefit of the picture. Ginns's temporal-contiguity meta-analysis, d = 0.87 (3.1). EVIDENCE.

**Several ideas in one sentence.** Long subordinate clauses, a participial opening, the subject and its verb far apart, the passive voice. NPR, Block, Gibson, the hearing-loss study (1.1 to 1.3). EVIDENCE (indirect) and PRACTICE.

**Too many numbers.** Unrounded figures, several in a sentence, or numbers with nothing to compare them to. NPR, Media Helping Media, the two-digit rule; Barrio and colleagues (1.6). EVIDENCE and PRACTICE.

**Wall-to-wall narration, or its opposite.** Narration with no room for the pictures; or slow, sparse narration that loses attention. Documentary practice and Attenborough (3.3); Rodero (1.7). PRACTICE and EVIDENCE.

**Interesting asides that are not the story.** Fun facts and tangents lower learning. Sundararajan and Adesope, g = -0.33; Muller's Extended Exposition (2.2, 2.5). EVIDENCE.

**Forms that exist only in writing.** Brackets, quotation marks, colons and semicolons (Media Helping Media and radio guides, 1.3; PRACTICE), and "the former and the latter", "respectively", "as mentioned above" (INFERENCE, 1.5).

**Negative constructions** that make the listener reverse the meaning. Radio guides (1.3). PRACTICE.

**Unexplained jargon and acronyms.** Radio guides (1.3). PRACTICE.

**Hype and exaggeration.** It buys no coverage (Sumner and colleagues), and a story persuades without justifying its claims (Dahlstrom) (2.3). EVIDENCE.

**A hedge on every verb.** It blurs which claims are firm. The evidence supports stating limits (Jensen; van der Bles and colleagues), not spreading qualifiers (2.4). INFERENCE.

**Purpose and intention language for evolutionary change.** "Evolved to", "learned to", "in order to", "so that". Kelemen; Nielson and colleagues; Sealey and Oakley (2.6). EVIDENCE, partly correlational.

**A late start.** The film's question arrives after the first few seconds. Meta and TikTok platform data (3.5). PLATFORM.

**Bad subtitles.** Broken in the middle of a phrase, too fast, or different from the narration. Gerber-Moron and colleagues; Liao and colleagues; Netflix, BBC, DCMP (4.1, 4.2). EVIDENCE and GUIDELINE.

**On-screen text that repeats whole narration sentences.** Kalyuga and colleagues; Adesope and Nesbit (4.3). EVIDENCE.

**A joke that does not land.** Humour helps only through mirth; an unfunny attempt can harm (2.2). EVIDENCE.

**A script never read aloud before recording.** NPR, Media Helping Media (1.2). PRACTICE.

**Pace not matched to density.** A dense sentence read fast, or thin filler read slowly. Rodero (1.7). EVIDENCE.

**An unrepresentative case presented as the result.** One dramatic creature standing in for what the population did. Dahlstrom's caution (2.3). INFERENCE from a review.

## 7. Tensions, gaps and open questions

**Captions against the redundancy principle.** Resolved in 4.3: subtitles follow the narration, for the sound-off and accessibility viewer; graphics carry key words, not sentences.

**"Do not describe the picture" against the audiovisual-redundancy studies.** Resolved in 3.2: the studies' redundancy means words and picture about the same event, not words restating the visible.

**Humour.** Engagement studies favour it; learning studies penalise detours. A joke on the point is safe; a joke off the point is not (2.2).

**Rhythm.** Short sentences are well supported; uniformly short ones sound clipped; I found no study of narration rhythm (1.2).

**Sound on or off.** TikTok's audience treats sound as essential; elsewhere much feed viewing is silent. The films have to work both ways (3.5).

**Where the evidence comes from.** The platform data are about advertisements and come from interested parties; the laboratory data are about lessons and news. Nothing I found tests the narration of short science films on social media directly. Rodero's pace figures are for Spanish, and the English voice-over figures are practice, not experiment.

**How much of this was read.** Many studies here were seen only as abstracts or search summaries (section 0). Where a decision rests on one of them (for example the exact effects in the humour studies, or the Attenborough quotations), reading the full source first would be worth the time.

## 8. The ten most useful, best-supported rules for our narration

1. **Open with the question the film answers, in the first sentence, within about three seconds.** PLATFORM (Meta; TikTok), with Welbourne and Grant's association of fast delivery with popularity.

2. **One idea per sentence.** Subject and verb early and close together, active voice, no clause the listener has to hold open, no participial opening; say what a thing is before naming it. EVIDENCE, indirect (Cowan; Gibson; the passive-voice study), and PRACTICE (NPR; Block).

3. **Every sentence is about what is on screen as it is heard, and says what the picture cannot:** what it is, why, what came before, how many generations, what changed. Never narrate what the viewer already sees. EVIDENCE (Drew and Grimes; Brosius and colleagues; Fox; Son and colleagues; Ginns's d = 0.87 for timing) and PRACTICE (Tompkins; Attenborough).

4. **Write to the cut and leave room.** About 140 to 150 words a minute, under-filling each gap, with the key visual moments left without words: roughly 100 to 130 words for a minute of film. This also keeps word-for-word subtitles readable. PRACTICE and GUIDELINE (voice-over rates; BBC and DCMP reading rates), with Rodero's evidence that pace and density must be matched.

5. **Few numbers:** rounded, one to a sentence, each with a comparison the viewer can picture, and units spoken in everyday words. EVIDENCE (Barrio, Goldstein and Hofman) and PRACTICE (NPR; Media Helping Media; the two-digit rule).

6. **Say what the viewer would expect before showing what happened.** EVIDENCE (Muller and colleagues: effect sizes of 0.79 and 0.83 over plain explanation).

7. **Speak to the viewer directly, in a conversational register:** contractions, plain words, first or second person; the maker can narrate as "I" or "we". Humour only when it is about the point, and only if it lands. EVIDENCE (Ginns, Martin and Marsh: retention d = .30, transfer d = .54; the humour and seductive-details studies), and correlational platform studies (Bunquin and Segre Cohen).

8. **State each limit once, plainly, in the narrator's own voice, where it applies; otherwise speak plainly; no hype.** EVIDENCE (Jensen; van der Bles and colleagues; Sumner and colleagues).

9. **Tell evolution as what happened to populations and lineages, not what creatures wanted, tried or needed.** No "evolved to", "learned to" or "in order to" for a change across generations; keep anthropomorphism for plain jokes. EVIDENCE, partly correlational (Kelemen; Nielson and colleagues; Sealey and Oakley).

10. **Subtitles word for word with the narration; graphics carry key words only.** Subtitles at most two lines of about 42 characters (fewer on a vertical frame), broken at phrase boundaries, at no more than 17 to 20 characters a second, and timed to shot changes. On-screen graphics carry key terms and numbers placed at what they name, never sentences repeating the narration. EVIDENCE (Gerber-Moron and colleagues; Liao and colleagues; Adesope and Nesbit; Mayer and Johnson) and GUIDELINE (Netflix; BBC; DCMP).

Behind all ten: read the script aloud before it is recorded, and have someone who has not seen the footage listen to it. PRACTICE (NPR; Media Helping Media).
