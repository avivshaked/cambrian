# What makes a short narrated science video compelling: research notes

Compiled on 2026-09-26 from web searches and the web pages they led to. The videos this is for are short narrated science stories. Each one tells what happened in one experiment of an artificial-life simulator, with footage of the simulated creatures, narration, subtitles and graphics, and is published on YouTube, YouTube Shorts, TikTok, Instagram Reels and similar platforms.

This document is the full record of what was found, written so that it can be used later without going back to the sources. Each source gets its own entry. The entry says what the source is and who made it, what it studied and how, what it found with the numbers, any key quotes, its limits, and what it means for these videos. Sections 8 to 11 draw the entries together: the common mistakes, the line between evidence and opinion, the gaps, and the ten best-supported principles. Section 12 lists every source with its type.

## 0. How to read this document

### 0.1 The evidence labels

Every entry carries one of four labels, which say what kind of source stands behind a claim.

**[Study]** is peer-reviewed or academic research: an experiment, a meta-analysis, a content analysis or a PhD thesis. One perspective article (Dahlstrom and Scheufele) is included under this label and marked as a perspective.

**[Platform]** is data or official guidance published by the platform itself: YouTube or Google, TikTok, Meta, Facebook or Instagram. The platforms have an interest in these numbers and do not publish their methods in full. Much of what they publish is about ads, not organic video.

**[Industry]** is data from a third party that is neither the platform nor an academic. That covers analytics vendors such as Socialinsider and Wistia, surveys run by media companies such as Verizon Media with Publicis Media, trade press such as Digiday and TechCrunch, and style standards such as Netflix's and the BBC's.

**[Practitioner]** is advice from working creators, strategists or writing teachers. It is opinion, however experienced the person giving it.

Where I apply a finding to these videos myself, the text says **(inference)**. None of the sources studied artificial-life stories, creature footage or anything close to it.

### 0.2 Method and its limits

The searches were run with a web search tool, and the key pages were fetched and read where the page allowed it. Some could not be read directly. Several PDFs came back as unreadable binary, some journal pages returned HTTP 403, and TikTok's Creator Academy loads its text by script. For those sources the entry rests on search-result summaries or secondary pages, and says so.

Three caveats apply to everything below. First, most hard numbers about hooks and length come from ads (Facebook with Nielsen, TikTok's ad data) or from brand and marketing video (Socialinsider, Wistia), not from organic science stories. Second, the multimedia-learning research (captions, voice, cues, redundancy) measures learning, not watch time or sharing: it says what makes a video understood and remembered, not what makes it spread. Third, engagement and understanding can come apart. Kulgemeyer and Wittwer (2022, entry 6.7) showed that viewers of a video full of misconceptions felt they understood as well as viewers of a correct one, so a video can be liked and still teach the wrong thing.

I found no study that measured story structure, hook type or length against retention for short narrated science videos specifically. Everything said here about that format is extrapolated from neighbouring evidence.

### 0.3 Claims that could not be verified at the source

Several figures are quoted widely on the web but could not be checked against their original source. They are collected here so that nobody later takes them for checked facts.

The most quoted is that 63% of the highest click-through TikTok ads show their key message in the first three seconds. Secondary pages attribute it to TikTok's ad data and agree it is about ads, not organic video, but I could not open TikTok's page that states it. A search summary also attributed to TikTok the claim that 90% of ad recall is captured in the first six seconds; the TikTok help page I did read says only that the hook belongs in the first six seconds and the proposition in the first three. TikTok's Creator Academy is reported to advise hooking viewers within the first five seconds, but its page loads by script and its text could not be read.

The figure that 79% of LinkedIn video is watched with the sound off is repeated in many marketing blogs; I found no LinkedIn source for it. Instagram's help centre is reported to allow Reels of up to 20 minutes and not to recommend Reels over three minutes to people who do not follow the account; this comes from Socialinsider and other third parties, and I did not read Instagram's own page. Adam Mosseri's three named ranking signals for Instagram (watch time, likes per reach, sends per reach, January 2025) are reported by marketing blogs; I did not see his original post.

Several benchmarks for YouTube's "viewed vs. swiped away" metric are vendor claims: that under 30% swipe-away is strong and 30 to 50% average, with under 30% earning four times the sustained distribution (ReelRise and similar pages), and that 70 to 90% viewed performed best while under 60% collapsed. One summary attached the second to Paddy Galloway's study, but I could not confirm it is his own figure.

Other vendor claims, all unverified and without a named method, are that hooks under two seconds had 23% higher completion than hooks of four to five seconds (said to come from a 2025 analysis of 10,000 viral TikToks); that TikTok's algorithm makes its first distribution decision around 1.5 seconds in; and a set of claims about animated word-by-word captions: that captions raise Reels completion from 37% to 65%, that animated formats deliver 41% higher engagement, and that 78.6% of 13.5 million clips used animated captions. No peer-reviewed evidence was found for or against word-by-word animated captions.

A search summary said a Meta study reported by Marketing Week found Reels ads with both music and voiceover scored 15 points higher on positive response than those without sound. I did not check it. Finally, Guo, Kim and Rubin's exact speaking-rate figure, often quoted as 254 words a minute, could not be read from their PDF; secondary summaries confirm the finding that faster speakers held viewers longer, but not that number.

## 1. The opening seconds: the hook

### 1.1 Facebook and Nielsen: most of an ad's value lands in the first seconds [Platform]

**What and who.** A page on Meta for Business titled "Capture Attention with Updated Features for Video Ads", reporting a Facebook study done with Nielsen. The page carries no date; its content is from around 2015 to 2016. https://www.facebook.com/business/news/updated-features-for-video-ads

**Method.** According to trade coverage, Nielsen pulled data from 173 of its Brand Effect studies that included video ads on Facebook, keeping only campaigns that showed a positive lift. The studies used a test-and-control design, and viewers in the test groups were split by how long they had watched (https://www.marketingdive.com/news/brand-lift-happens-in-less-than-1-second-of-video-study-finds/377333/ and https://martech.org/even-brief-video-views-drive-brand-lift-facebook-nielsen-study-finds/).

**Findings.** "Up to 47% of the value in a video campaign was delivered in the first three seconds, while up to 74% of the value was delivered in the first ten." Lift in ad recall, brand awareness and purchase consideration appeared from the moment an ad was seen, even under one second: viewers who watched three seconds or less showed lifts of 47% in ad recall, 32% in brand awareness and 44% in purchase intent. The same page says that "65% of people who watch the first three seconds of a video will watch for at least ten seconds and 45% continue watching for thirty seconds". It also reports that people watch over 100 million hours of video on Facebook a day and "consume content on Facebook faster on mobile than on desktop". The page's figures on captions and loud audio are in entries 7.1 and 7.15.

**Limits.** These are ads measured for brand effects, not stories measured for understanding or sharing. The sample kept only campaigns that worked. The data are about ten years old and from Facebook's feed, where most viewing was muted.

**What it means here (inference).** Whatever the viewer takes away from a feed video, much of it is decided in the first seconds, and most viewers who get past the first three seconds stay for ten. The first frames should already carry the story's point: the strange outcome, or the question.

### 1.2 TikTok's ad help centre: the hook in six seconds, the point in three [Platform]

**What and who.** TikTok's "Creative best practices for performance ads" in its ad help centre, undated. https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices

**Findings and quotes.** "Prioritize your hook in the first 6 seconds to boost engagement and increase watch time" and "Introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds for better recall and awareness." It recommends the order hook, then unique selling points, then a clear call to action that tells the audience "what to think, feel, or do". It asks for vertical 9:16 video at 720p or more, sound or music, content kept "visible within the UI safe zone", text overlays "displaying 5-10 words per second when using text", people on screen (creators, employees, customers), a do-it-yourself look rather than polished production, three to five creatives per ad group, and a refresh of the creatives when results decline.

**Limits.** This is guidance for ads, and the page gives no data for its claims.

**What it means here (inference).** The platform tells its own advertisers to state the point within three seconds. For a science story the equivalent of "the proposition" is the question or the surprising result. The words-per-second figure is questioned in entry 7.8.

### 1.3 TikTok's "63%" figure [Platform, reported second-hand]

The claim is that more than 63% of the highest click-through TikTok ads highlight their key message or product within the first three seconds. Secondary pages attribute it to TikTok's own ads creative library and agree it is about ads, not organic video (https://hansencommerce.com/insights-tiktok-hook-3-seconds, https://lebesgue.io/tiktok-ads/how-to-increase-tiktok-ctr-9-creative-tips). I could not open the TikTok page that states it, so it is unverified. If it holds, it points the same way as entries 1.1 and 1.2.

### 1.4 TikTok Creator Academy: hook within five seconds [Practitioner, from the platform; page unreadable]

TikTok's Creator Academy article on the elements of a TikTok video (https://www.tiktok.com/creator-academy/en/article/elements-of-tiktok-video?lang=en) is reported in search results to say that "no matter how long or short your video is, hooking the viewer within the first 5 seconds is incredibly important", and to recommend an attractive element early to stop viewers scrolling. The page loads by script and its text could not be read directly. It is the only guidance found that TikTok addresses to creators rather than to advertisers.

### 1.5 YouTube's retention report and its "Intro" figure [Platform]

**What and who.** YouTube's help page on key moments for audience retention. https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415

**Content.** "Intro tells you what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds." "Spikes are moments in your video that were rewatched or shared." "Dips highlight moments in your video that were either skipped or moments where viewers stopped watching your video completely." "Typical retention" compares a video with the channel's ten latest videos of similar length, and with all YouTube videos of similar length. Trade coverage of the 2020 redesign describes the same four key moments (intro, continuous segments, spikes, dips): https://www.tubefilter.com/2020/10/02/youtube-viewer-retention-analytics-creator-insider/

**Limits.** It is a measuring tool. It says what YouTube shows creators, not what YouTube ranks on or with what weight.

**What it means here (inference).** YouTube's own instrument singles out the first 30 seconds of a long video and marks where people skip or leave. Reading the dips of each published story against its script is the cheapest test of which beats lose people.

### 1.6 YouTube Shorts: "viewed vs. swiped away" [Platform]

**What and who.** A YouTube community video announcing the metric. https://support.google.com/youtube/community-video/273390203/new-youtube-shorts-metric-viewed-vs-swiped-away?hl=en

**Content.** The metric shows how often a Short was shown in the Shorts feed and whether viewers chose to watch it or swipe past it. It is available in YouTube Studio on the web and on mobile, in advanced and content-level analytics.

**Limits.** YouTube does not publish how much the metric counts in ranking. Vendor explainers call it among the most important Shorts ranking factors (https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-shorts-algorithm/, https://buffer.com/resources/the-creators-guide-to-youtube-shorts-analytics/, https://reelrise.app/guide/viewed-vs-swiped-away-the-only-youtube-shorts-metric-that-matters/), which is opinion. The benchmark figures quoted for it are unverified (section 0.3).

**What it means here (inference).** On Shorts the decision to stay is made before any story has started. The opening frame has to work the way a thumbnail does on long-form YouTube.

### 1.7 Curiosity improves memory: Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath (2014) [Study]

**What and who.** "States of curiosity modulate hippocampus-dependent learning via the dopaminergic circuit", by Matthias Gruber, Bernard Gelman and Charan Ranganath, *Neuron*, 2014. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25284006/ and https://www.cell.com/neuron/fulltext/S0896-6273(14)00804-6

**Method.** Participants read trivia questions in an fMRI scanner, rated how curious they were about each answer, and were shown unrelated faces while they waited for the answers. Memory was tested immediately and again one day later.

**Findings.** In both tests people remembered the answers they had been curious about better, and they also remembered better the unrelated faces shown to them while curious. High curiosity raised activity in the midbrain and the nucleus accumbens, the brain's reward circuit, and strengthened its coupling with the hippocampus, which the authors read as dopamine helping memories to form. A later review builds a framework on this result (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6891259/).

**Limits.** Trivia in a laboratory, with the small samples typical of brain-imaging studies. The outcome is memory, not watching.

**What it means here (inference).** A question opened at the start does two things: it gives the viewer a reason to wait for the answer, and it makes what they see while waiting more memorable. It supports opening on a puzzle rather than on a statement.

### 1.8 Predicting before the reveal: Brod, Hasselhorn and Bunge (2018) [Study]

**What and who.** "When generating a prediction boosts learning: The element of surprise", by Garvin Brod, Marcus Hasselhorn and Silvia Bunge, *Learning and Instruction* 55, 22-31, 2018. https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0qn99739

**Method.** Two tasks, with behavioural measures and eye tracking, including pupil dilation as a measure of surprise.

**Findings.** In the first task, asking people to predict an outcome, rather than to judge it after seeing it, improved how much geography they learned. Outcomes that broke the prediction produced a surprise response only when a prediction had been made first, and the stronger that response the more was learned. In the second task, predicting the results of soccer matches improved memory specifically for the unexpected results. The authors conclude that the surprise following a wrong prediction is a key reason predicting helps. A later review in *Psychonomic Bulletin and Review* covers predicting as a learning strategy more broadly: https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-021-01904-1

**Limits.** Laboratory tasks with factual content. A video cannot make a viewer predict; it can only invite them to.

**What it means here (inference).** Before the reveal, the narration can ask the viewer what they think the creatures did. A result that breaks their guess is then remembered better. This suits a simulator, where outcomes are often not what anyone expected.

### 1.9 Giving away the ending: the spoiler studies [Study]

**What and who.** Jonathan Leavitt and Nicholas Christenfeld, "Story spoilers don't spoil stories", *Psychological Science*, 2011 (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797611417007), and the studies that tried to reproduce it.

**Findings.** Leavitt and Christenfeld gave readers plot-crucial information before a short story and found the spoiled stories were enjoyed as much as, or more than, the unspoiled ones. Later work did not hold this up. Johnson and Rosenbaum (2015, *Communication Research*, "Spoiler alert: consequences of narrative spoilers for dimensions of enjoyment, appreciation, and transportation") and Levine, Betzner and Autry found that spoilers reduced enjoyment. A University of Arkansas honours thesis reviewing the field describes the results as mixed, some positive, some negative and some showing no effect, and calls Leavitt and Christenfeld's finding an outlier (https://scholarworks.uark.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1009&context=psycuht). Daniel and Katz (2019, *Psychological Reports*) found spoilers affected enjoyment of television episodes but not of short stories (https://doi.org/10.1177/0033294118793971).

**Limits.** These studies test fiction read or watched for enjoyment, not science explanations.

**What it means here (inference).** Whether to state the experiment's outcome in the hook is not settled by the evidence: it is neither proven harmless nor proven harmful. What the curiosity evidence does support is leaving a question open. A hook that says what happened but not why keeps the question open while still showing the result.

### 1.10 Why YouTube stopped ranking on clicks alone [Platform]

**What and who.** "On YouTube's recommendation system", a YouTube Blog post by Cristos Goodrow, YouTube's Vice President of Engineering, 15 September 2021. https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/

**Content.** The system uses six kinds of signal: "clicks, watchtime, survey responses, sharing, likes, and dislikes". In 2012 YouTube found that a click did not mean the viewer watched the video. The example given is clicking what looks like Wimbledon highlights, finding commentary instead, and clicking again until finding the real thing. So YouTube added watch time. Because some watch time is time people later regret, it then added "valued watchtime", defined as "the time spent watching a video that you consider valuable". Viewers are surveyed and rate videos from one to five stars; only four- and five-star ratings count, and a model predicts the rating for everyone who was not surveyed.

**Limits.** A description by the platform, without weights.

**What it means here (inference).** Watching and satisfaction are both measured. A hook that draws people in, followed by a video that disappoints them, is scored as a disappointment.

### 1.11 YouTube's help page on how recommendations work [Platform]

YouTube's help page "How YouTube recommendations work" (https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16089387?hl=en-GB) lists the signals from the viewer's side: watch history, search history, channel subscriptions, likes and dislikes, "not interested" feedback, "don't recommend channel" choices, and satisfaction surveys, which "help the system understand satisfaction, not just watch time". It says the Shorts feed is personalised to what the system thinks the viewer wants to see next, without saying how "viewed vs. swiped away" is used, and it gives no advice to creators. Its relevance here is that it confirms, on the platform's current help page as well as in the 2021 post, that satisfaction surveys feed ranking.

### 1.12 YouTube removes "egregious clickbait" [Platform]

**What and who.** A Google India blog post, "Strengthening enforcement against egregious clickbait on YouTube", 18 December 2024. https://blog.google/intl/en-in/products/platforms/strengthening-enforcement-against-egregious-clickbait-on-youtube/ (coverage: https://www.medianama.com/2024/12/223-youtube-to-begin-crackdown-on-egregious-clickbait-india/)

**Content.** Egregious clickbait is a title or thumbnail that makes promises or claims the video does not deliver, especially about breaking news or current events, which can leave viewers "feeling tricked, frustrated, or even misled". The example given is a title saying "the president resigned!" on a video that never addresses a resignation. Such videos are removed, without a strike against the channel. Enforcement began in India, with other regions to follow.

**What it means here (inference).** The hook, the title and the thumbnail make one promise. For a science story the risk is overstating the finding: "creatures evolve intelligence" on a video about a slightly better swimming stroke.

### 1.13 Clickbait and credibility: Molyneux and Coddington (2020) [Study]

**What and who.** Logan Molyneux and Mark Coddington, "Aggregation, clickbait and their effect on perceptions of journalistic credibility and quality", *Journalism Practice* 14(4), 429-446, 2020. https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Aggregation,-Clickbait-and-Their-Effect-on-of-and-Molyneux-Coddington/b07bc3ca25119d65a499c29cba702ae1fa3728ee and https://scholarshare.temple.edu/handle/20.500.12613/393

**Method.** Two online experiments using published original and aggregated news articles as stimuli.

**Findings.** Aggregation itself had little effect on perceived credibility and quality; how well the piece was written mattered more. The results suggest clickbait headlines may lower perceived credibility and quality. The authors argue the damage builds up over time, each time a clickbait headline disappoints, until readers stop trusting the source.

**Limits.** News articles and headlines, not video; the clickbait effect is stated as a suggestion, not a firm result.

**What it means here (inference).** A science channel's main asset is being believed. A hook that oversells a result may win one view and cost the next.

### 1.14 Opening on a mystery: Cialdini (2005) [Practitioner]

**What and who.** Robert Cialdini, "What's the best secret device for engaging student interest? The answer is in the title", *Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology* 24(1), 2005. https://guilfordjournals.com/doi/10.1521/jscp.24.1.22.59166 . Cialdini is a social psychologist; this is an essay on teaching, not an experiment, so it is labelled practitioner.

**Content.** Reviewing successful science writing, Cialdini found that the best pieces began with a mystery story: the author described a state of affairs that seemed to make no sense and invited the reader into the rest of the piece as the way of solving it. He argues that a description demands attention and a question demands an answer, but a mystery demands something more valuable, an explanation, and that working through the explanation gives the best chance of understanding that lasts.

**Limits.** One expert's reading of what works, without a test.

**What it means here (inference).** An artificial-life experiment often produces exactly this: a result that makes no sense until the mechanism is shown. That makes the mystery the natural shape for these stories, with the explanation as the payoff.

### 1.15 Good and bad clickbait: Derek Muller (2021) [Practitioner]

**What and who.** "Clickbait is Unreasonably Effective", a Veritasium video by Derek Muller, 17 August 2021. https://www.imdb.com/title/tt15251060/ (discussion: https://yahnd.com/theater/r/youtube/S2xHZPH5Sng/)

**Content.** Muller argues that the title and thumbnail play a huge part in a video's success. He separates good clickbait, "legitbait", titles and images that attract attention and deliver what they promise, from bad clickbait, "clicktraps", which get the click and lead to content of little value. He says he began bad at titles and thumbnails and improved through YouTube's analytics and his own testing.

**Limits.** One creator's view, if a successful science creator's.

**What it means here (inference).** Making a title and thumbnail compelling is compatible with honesty, provided the video delivers exactly what they promise.

### 1.16 The MrBeast production memo (leaked 2024) [Practitioner]

**What and who.** "How to Succeed in MrBeast Production", a 36-page internal onboarding document said to be written by Jimmy Donaldson (MrBeast), which leaked in September 2024. https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/ (coverage: https://cybernews.com/news/mrbeast-leaked-pdf-spills-secrets-how-to-be-successful-on-youtube/)

**Content.** Staff are told to focus on three numbers: click-through rate (the share of people shown the thumbnail who click it), average view duration and average view percentage. The first minute of a video is called the most important; it "has to match expectations from thumbnail" and hook viewers at once. Minutes one to three move from the hype of the opening to the execution, using "crazy progression" so that the viewer becomes "super invested". The stated goal is "to make the best YOUTUBE videos possible".

**Limits.** Written for large-budget entertainment made for long-form YouTube, not for science; it is advice, not data.

**What it means here (inference).** The two ideas that carry over are that the opening must deliver on the thumbnail's promise, and that once the hook has worked the story must visibly move forward rather than set up at length.

### 1.17 Paddy Galloway's study of 3.3 billion Shorts views (2023) [Practitioner]

**What and who.** A thread by Paddy Galloway, a YouTube strategist, April 2023. https://www.linkedin.com/posts/paddy-galloway-459b8913a_we-studied-33-billion-views-to-decode-the-activity-7053697597592498176-PAkf and https://x.com/PaddyG96/status/1646898356419981315 (summary: https://videogen.io/blog/decoding-the-youtube-shorts-algorithm-a-deep-dive-into-3-3-billion-views)

**Method.** His team looked at about 5,400 Shorts from 33 channels, with 3.3 billion views between them, using the channels' own analytics.

**Findings.** The Shorts algorithm appeared to favour videos of 40 seconds or more that held viewers well, though there were outliers at every length. Because of "viewed vs. swiped away", the first second needs to be punchy, and the intro should be treated like a thumbnail. Likes, shares and comments did not seem very important, though he planned more tests. Long-form videos still converted viewers into subscribers better per view than Shorts, though Shorts helped channels grow.

**Limits.** An observational look at a chosen set of channels, by a consultant, without a published method; YouTube changes the system often.

**What it means here (inference).** A story of 40 seconds or more is not penalised on Shorts if it holds; the first second decides whether it is seen at all.

### 1.18 Intro length on YouTube [Practitioner]

Creator guides advise replacing long animated logo intros with a second or two, and say that anything longer than about five seconds before the value arrives flattens the retention curve (https://www.creator-playbook.com/articles/your-intro-sucks-fix-first-30-seconds, https://gyre.pro/blog/how-to-make-a-youtube-intro-that-keeps-viewers-watching, https://1of10.com/blog/how-to-hook-viewers-in-the-first-30-seconds-of-a-youtube-video/). These pages attribute quotes to YouTube's Creator Insider channel ("make that clear in 5 seconds or lose the viewer") that I could not trace to the channel. The advice is consistent with the platform data in entries 1.1 and 1.5 but is itself opinion.

### 1.19 Show the result first [Practitioner]

Guides to Shorts suggest opening on the end state: a fitness creator shows the "after" shot first, a finance creator opens with the mistake and then explains the fix, a commentary creator starts with the contrary line (https://joinbrands.com/blog/youtube-shorts-best-practices/). They also describe the hook as creating an "information gap" that makes the viewer want what comes next. This is the practitioner version of the curiosity evidence in entry 1.7.

### 1.20 What the hook evidence adds up to

The platform data (1.1 to 1.6) agree that the first seconds decide most of what follows, but that evidence is mostly about ads. The studies (1.7 to 1.9) give the mechanism: an open question and a prediction that can be broken make people attend and remember, while giving away the ending has no settled effect. The platforms (1.10 to 1.12) and one experiment (1.13) show that a hook the video does not deliver on is penalised, by ranking, by removal and by lost trust. The practitioners (1.14 to 1.19) agree on opening with the result or the mystery at once and without branding. (inference) For a simulator story: open in the first one to three seconds on the footage of the surprising outcome, state the question it raises, and deliver exactly that answer.

## 2. Retention and pacing

### 2.1 Course videos: Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014) [Study]

**What and who.** Philip Guo, Juho Kim and Rob Rubin, "How video production affects student engagement: an empirical study of MOOC videos", Proceedings of the first ACM conference on Learning at Scale, 2014, pages 41-50. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2556325.2566239 (abstract text: https://pg.ucsd.edu/publications/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.txt)

**Method.** 6.9 million video-watching sessions across four edX courses, the largest study of video engagement at the time. Engagement was measured as how long students watched each video and whether they then attempted the exercises that followed it. That is engagement, not learning.

**Findings.** Shorter videos were much more engaging; the median engagement time was at most about six minutes whatever the video's length, and attention fell off for longer videos. Informal talking-head videos were more engaging than others, and videos that alternated between the instructor's head and the slides held students better than slides alone. Khan-style tablet drawing was more engaging than slides. High-quality pre-recorded classroom lectures might not make engaging online videos. Students engaged most with fast-speaking instructors, which the authors attribute to enthusiasm rather than speed as such. Students also engaged differently with lecture videos and tutorial videos. The numbers come through secondary summaries because the PDF could not be read: https://oscqr.suny.edu/how-long-should-instructional-videos-be/ and https://blogs.ubc.ca/videolearninghub/2019/08/04/how-video-production-affects-student-engagement-an-empirical-study-of-mooc-videos/

**Limits.** Students who chose to take a course, watching lecture videos; engagement, not learning or sharing.

**What it means here (inference).** Energy in the delivery and switching between a presence and the explanatory picture hold attention. These videos have no instructor on screen, so the narrator's energy and the cuts between creature footage and graphics are what carry this.

### 2.2 Science on YouTube: Welbourne and Grant (2016) [Study]

**What and who.** Dustin Welbourne and Will Grant (Australian National University), "Science communication on YouTube: Factors that affect channel and video popularity", *Public Understanding of Science*, 2016. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963662515572068 and https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272518559_Science_communication_on_YouTube_Factors_that_affect_channel_and_video_popularity

**Method.** A content analysis of 390 videos from 39 YouTube science channels, comparing features of the videos with their popularity.

**Findings.** Professionally produced videos were more numerous, but videos made by independent creators ("user-generated content") were significantly more popular, and their delivery was significantly quicker. Videos from channels with a consistent science communicator were more popular than those without a regular one; the authors suggest a regular host gives the audience someone to connect with. A fast pace may help focus the audience and make the message more persuasive.

**Limits.** Correlational: it cannot say that pace or a host causes popularity. It predates Shorts, TikTok and Reels.

**What it means here (inference).** A recognisable, consistent voice across the series, and quick delivery, are the two features most likely to transfer. With no presenter on screen, the same narrator and the same visual identity in every story are the closest substitute for a regular host.

### 2.3 Pacing and arousing content: Lang, Bolls, Potter and Kawahara (1999) [Study]

**What and who.** Annie Lang, Paul Bolls, Robert Potter and K. Kawahara, "The effects of production pacing and arousing content on the information processing of television messages", *Journal of Broadcasting and Electronic Media* 43, 1999. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08838159909364504

**Method.** Viewers watched television messages that varied in production pacing (how often the picture cut) and in arousing content, with self-reported and physiological measures, within the limited-capacity model of media processing.

**Findings.** Both fast pacing and arousing content raised self-reported arousal, though through different physiological patterns, and both increased the processing resources viewers gave to the message.

**Limits.** Television messages from the 1990s; the paper is read here through its abstract and summaries.

**What it means here (inference).** Cutting more often draws more attention, up to a point that the next study locates.

### 2.4 Too fast and too exciting at once: "Slow Down You're Moving Too Fast" (2015) [Study]

**What and who.** "Slow Down You're Moving Too Fast: Age, Production Pacing, Arousing Content, and Memory for Television Messages", *Journal of Media Psychology* 27(2), 2015 (from Lang's research group). https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1027/1864-1105/a000130 and https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276338153_Slow_Down_You're_Moving_Too_Fast

**Method.** Younger and older adults watched the same set of messages, chosen to vary in pacing, arousing content and emotional valence; attention, arousal and memory were measured.

**Findings.** The combination of fast pacing and arousing content overloaded viewers' processing, and they recognised and recalled less of the specific content. Older and younger adults remembered slow-paced messages equally well, but as pacing rose younger adults did increasingly better than older ones, and more so for arousing messages. Older adults paid more attention to all messages, and that attention did not turn into better memory.

**Limits.** Television messages; memory for content, not whether people kept watching.

**What it means here (inference).** Fast cutting keeps attention, but fast cutting on top of a dramatic moment reduces how much of the explanation is remembered. The practical rule is to cut quickly through the calm stretches and slow down at the moment where the drama and the explanation arrive together, which in these stories is usually the reveal. Older viewers are hit hardest.

### 2.5 Shot lengths and attention in Hollywood film: Cutting, DeLong and Nothelfer (2010) [Study]

**What and who.** James Cutting, Jordan DeLong and Christine Nothelfer, "Attention and the Evolution of Hollywood Film", *Psychological Science*, 2010. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797610361679

**Method.** 150 films released between 1935 and 2005 were broken into their sequences of shots, and the pattern of shot lengths in each film was analysed.

**Findings.** Over the 70 years, the length of each shot became more correlated with the lengths of its neighbours, and the pattern approached the "1/f" spectrum that also describes fluctuations in human attention and reaction times. Action films came closest, followed by adventure, animation, comedy and drama. The authors suggest such a shot structure may help hold viewers' attention on the narrative.

**Limits.** Descriptive: it shows how films changed, not that the change works better. Feature films, not short videos.

**What it means here (inference).** Rhythm matters as well as speed: runs of short shots and runs of longer ones, varying gradually, rather than one fixed cutting rate.

### 2.6 TikTok's 2021 ad-creative analysis: scenes, length, text and voice [Platform]

**What and who.** "Triple-digit conversion lift: Simple creative tips to make your ads work harder", TikTok for Business blog, 21 December 2021. https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/creative-that-drives-conversions

**Method.** TikTok says it used "a machine learning model to analyze the performance of thousands of ads". No further method is given.

**Findings.** In e-commerce ads, "variety of scenes led to a 38% lift in conversion" over one person selling a product in a continuous shot; in gaming ads, "five or more scenes showed an incredible 171% lift in conversion". Ads of "21 and 34 seconds in length received a 280% lift in conversion" over shorter or longer ones. In e-commerce, ads using "closed captioning or on-screen text" that clearly showed an offer saw "an 80% lift in conversion", and a "human voiceover" combined with a written offer "saw an 87% lift". In gaming, "onscreen text in the first 7 seconds saw a 43% lift in conversion".

**Limits.** Ads measured by conversions (a purchase or install), not stories measured by watching or understanding. Correlations picked out by a model, with no controls described. Published by the platform to encourage advertisers.

**What it means here (inference).** Several scenes rather than one shot, a human-sounding voice, and text early on all went with better results even in ads. These are weak but consistent pointers for the story format; the length figure is for selling, not for telling.

### 2.7 Practitioner advice on pacing [Practitioner]

Creators widely advise changing the picture every few seconds ("pattern interrupts") and re-hooking the viewer partway through ("rehooks"), and the MrBeast memo (entry 1.16) asks for "crazy progression" after the opening. I found no study that tests a particular interval between changes. The evidence in 2.3 to 2.5 supports frequent change for attention, with the caution from 2.4 about the moment of explanation.

### 2.8 What the pacing evidence adds up to

Quick delivery, a lively voice and frequent visual change go with longer watching (2.1, 2.2, 2.6) and with more attention (2.3). But speed on top of a dramatic moment costs memory for the content (2.4), and good film rhythm varies rather than holding one rate (2.5). (inference) Cut briskly through the setting and the build-up; hold the shot and slow the narration at the reveal and the explanation; keep one narrator's voice across the series.

## 3. Length

### 3.1 YouTube Shorts can be three minutes long [Platform]

YouTube announced on 3 October 2024, in the blog post "Tall updates coming to Shorts", that from 15 October 2024 creators could upload Shorts of up to three minutes, calling it a top request from creators ("more flexibility to tell your story"). The limit applies to videos that are square or taller, and videos uploaded before that date are unaffected. https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/tall-updates-coming-to-shorts/ and https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877?hl=en . (inference) A story of 60 to 90 seconds fits Shorts easily; the limit is no longer a reason to cut a story short.

### 3.2 YouTube Shorts count every replay as a view from 31 March 2025 [Platform, via trade press]

According to TechCrunch (26 March 2025) and a support note from Sprout Social, from 31 March 2025 a Shorts view is counted every time a Short starts or replays, with no minimum watch time, while a separate "engaged views" figure, plays that last past the first seconds, is what counts for monetisation and the Partner Programme. https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/26/youtube-is-changing-how-youtube-shorts-views-are-counted and https://support.sproutsocial.com/hc/en-us/articles/35874991211533-YouTube-Shorts-View-Count-Update-March-2025 . Practitioner pages infer that endings which flow back into the opening, so the Short loops, now earn more views (https://virvid.ai/blog/looping-structure-shorts-retention-2026); that inference is opinion. (inference) View counts on Shorts after that date are not comparable with earlier ones, and engaged views are the better measure.

### 3.3 Instagram Reels go to three minutes and are recommended at that length [Platform, via trade press]

Adam Mosseri, head of Instagram, announced on 19 January 2025 that Reels could be up to three minutes long, up from 90 seconds, after creators said 90 seconds was too short: "We're hoping that upping that limit up to three minutes will help you tell the stories that you really want to tell." (https://www.medianama.com/2025/01/223-instagram-reels-3-minutes-us-tiktok-ban/). Social Media Today reported that this reversed Instagram's position of May 2024, when it advised creators against longer Reels because people engaged with them less and did not recommend longer clips in its discovery surfaces; in January 2025 it said it would recommend longer Reels in Explore as it does shorter ones, because "appetite around long-form content is changing" (https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-will-recommend-longer-3-minute-reels/737913/). Reports that Instagram's help centre now allows up to 20 minutes, but does not recommend Reels over three minutes to non-followers, are unverified (section 0.3; https://www.inro.social/blog/instagram-reels-can-now-be-20-minutes-long-new-time-limit-explained-2025). (inference) Keep Reels under three minutes if the aim is to reach people who do not already follow the account.

### 3.4 TikTok's limits and its one-minute rule for pay [Platform, reported second-hand]

TikTok accepts uploaded videos of up to ten minutes; recording inside the app offers presets from 15 seconds to three minutes, and an earlier test of ten-minute in-app recording ended (https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/You-Can-Still-Upload-10-Minute-Videos-on-TikTok/648013/, https://streamlabs.com/content-hub/post/how-to-upload-ten-minute-tiktok). Its Creator Rewards Program pays only on qualified views of original videos at least one minute long, and a video begins earning after 1,000 qualified For You views (https://creatorsagency.co/blog/tiktok-creator-rewards-program-2026, https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/internet/tiktok-creators-will-soon-able-make-money-subscriptions-rcna141946). These come from third-party pages summarising TikTok's rules. (inference) If income from TikTok matters, a story has to run at least 60 seconds there; if it does not, the length can follow the story.

### 3.5 Instagram Reels length and performance: Socialinsider (2026) [Industry]

**What and who.** Socialinsider, an analytics company, "What Is the Ideal Instagram Reels Length?". https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/instagram-reels-length/

**Method.** 6 million Reels from brands with an active presence on Instagram between January and June 2026, grouped by length.

**Findings.** Reels of 45 to 60 seconds had the highest engagement rate (0.35%) and the highest median views (10,374). Past 180 seconds engagement fell to 0.15% and median views to 4,428, roughly the level of clips under 30 seconds. Reels under 30 seconds did worse than the 45 to 60 second range on both measures. The article also states that Reels over three minutes are not placed in the Reels tab and reach mainly existing followers.

**Limits.** Brand accounts, not science creators; observational, so long Reels may do worse partly because of who makes them; engagement rate as defined by the vendor.

**What it means here (inference).** On Reels, the middle range of about 45 to 60 seconds did best, and very short clips did not win. That is room for a complete short story.

### 3.6 Video length and engagement: Wistia (2025 and earlier) [Industry]

**What and who.** Wistia, a video-hosting company for businesses, "How to Choose the Right Marketing Video Length for Any Goal", drawing on its "2026 State of Video Report", published 1 July 2025. https://wistia.com/learn/marketing/optimal-video-length

**Method.** Over 13 million videos uploaded to Wistia, mostly marketing, product and training videos embedded on websites.

**Findings.** "Videos under a minute average a 52% engagement rate", and viewers "typically watch over 50% of an educational video and a tutorial video" of one to five minutes. Wistia recommends under one minute for social awareness, one to five minutes for educational content, five to 30 minutes for conversion and 30 minutes or more for training. Older Wistia figures, reported by secondary pages and not checked against Wistia's own, are that retention was about 70% for videos under two minutes and below 50% for longer ones; that the average loss in the opening was 4.9% for videos of one to two minutes against 17.3% for videos of five to ten minutes; that engagement changed little between one and five minutes and fell past five; that a 2023 version put the drop at about 60 seconds; and that in 2025 videos of a minute or less reached 50% engagement against 38% for those over five minutes (https://cloudinary.com/guides/marketing-videos/optimal-video-length-strategies-for-maximizing-viewer-retention, https://wistia.com/learn/marketing/understanding-audience-retention).

**Limits.** Business video on websites, not social feeds; "engagement" is the share of the video watched on average.

**What it means here (inference).** In a different setting from social feeds, educational videos of one to five minutes still held viewers for over half their length. A longer YouTube version of a story is not doomed by length alone.

### 3.7 TikTok ads of 21 to 34 seconds [Platform]

TikTok's 2021 analysis (entry 2.6) found ads of 21 to 34 seconds had a 280% lift in conversions over shorter or longer ones. It is ad data measured by sales, published by the platform, and it is the only length figure TikTok gives. It says little about how long a story should be.

### 3.8 One science account on TikTok and Instagram: Habibi and Salim (2021) [Study]

**What and who.** Sarah Habibi and Lidya Salim, "Static vs. dynamic methods of delivery for science communication: A critical analysis of user engagement with science on social media", *PLoS ONE* 16(3): e0248507, 31 March 2021. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8011769/

**Method.** One science-communication account: 40 TikTok videos posted over six weeks (May to June 2020) and 20 Instagram posts over one year. Likes, comments, shares, saves and views were taken from the platforms' analytics, expressed as a share of reach, and compared with paired t-tests.

**Findings.** On Instagram, videos with an experiment in them drew many more shares (2.27% of reach against 0.24% for static posts) and saves (1.87% against 0.36%); the authors conclude that "users interact with ESC significantly more (p<0.05) when the content is presented in dynamic ways with a component of experimentation". On TikTok, 15-second videos had the highest completion, 69% against 28 to 48% for longer formats, and the authors advise that a communicator who wants videos watched to the end "should keep the videos under 15 seconds". But the long experiment videos of 40 to 60 seconds had by far the most average views, about 248,819 against 29,576 to 55,914 for lecture-style videos. Both kinds of Instagram post carried captions of 200 to 400 words describing the content.

**Limits.** One account, a small number of posts, a short period in 2020, and no control for topic or timing.

**What it means here (inference).** Two things pull against each other: short videos are completed more often, while longer videos that show something happening, an experiment, gathered far more views. Simulator footage of creatures doing something is the "experiment" kind of content, which is encouraging for 40 to 60 seconds and longer.

### 3.9 What the length evidence adds up to

No source supports a single ideal length. Shorter videos are finished more often (3.8, 3.6), while longer ones can earn more total watching and more views when each beat pays off (3.8, 1.17, 3.5). The platforms now allow three minutes on Shorts and Reels and ten on TikTok, with rules that favour at least one minute on TikTok and at most three on Reels for reaching new viewers. (inference) For one experiment: one question, about 45 to 90 seconds on the vertical platforms (at least 60 on TikTok if income matters, under three minutes on Reels), and a longer YouTube version only when the story has more than one turn.

## 4. How the platforms differ

This section gathers the sources about individual platforms that are not already covered above, then describes each platform in turn.

### 4.1 Sound on TikTok: the Kantar research [Platform]

**What and who.** Research by Kantar, commissioned and published by TikTok for Business in the blog posts "Kantar report: How brands are making noise and driving impact with sound on TikTok" and "Evolution of Sound, Volume 1". https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en/blog/kantar-report-how-brands-are-making-noise-and-driving-impact-with-sound-on-tiktok and https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/evolution-of-sound-volume-1 (coverage: https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/tiktok-shares-new-insights-into-the-importance-of-sound-for-marketing-promo/601569/)

**Findings.** 88% of TikTok users said sound is essential to the TikTok experience. 73% said they would "stop and look" at ads on TikTok that had audio, which TikTok says is higher than on any other platform. Users experienced sound on TikTok as "fun", a 66% increase over other platforms with the audio off, and as more informative, appealing and engaging, while on other platforms they tended to find sound distracting or even annoying. A study the year before found brand linkage and recall rose more than eightfold when distinctive brand sounds were used, compared with slogans and logos.

**Limits.** A survey commissioned by the platform, about ads; the methods are not given on the blog.

**What it means here (inference).** On TikTok the narration and the sound design are heard by most viewers. The narration can carry the story there, with captions as support.

### 4.2 Sound on Instagram Reels [Platform]

Instagram for Business posted that "80% of people view Reels with sound on", citing Instagram's internal data from February 2022 (https://www.facebook.com/instagramforbusiness/posts/80-of-people-view-reels-with-sound-on-try-one-of-these-5-great-songs-on-metas-fr/758319112755506/). Meta's developer blog post "Unlock the Power of Reels Ads: Boosting Brand Awareness and Engagement", 7 November 2024, says "Over 75% of Reels views on Instagram are sound on", and recommends using voiceover, music, ASMR or sound effects, vertical 9:16 video and a safe zone for key messages, without giving the safe zone's dimensions (https://developers.facebook.com/blog/post/2024/11/07/unlock-the-power-of-reel-ads/). Both are the platform's own figures without a method. (inference) Reels are a sound-on place, like TikTok, unlike the Facebook feed.

### 4.3 Instagram's ranking signals, as reported from Mosseri [Industry, reporting the platform]

Marketing blogs report that in January 2025 Adam Mosseri named the three signals that matter most for Reels: watch time (including replays), likes per reach, and sends per reach, meaning how often a Reel is shared by direct message relative to how many people saw it. They report that sends weigh more for reaching people who do not follow the account and likes more for followers, one of them putting sends at three to five times the weight of likes for non-followers (https://www.dataslayer.ai/blog/instagram-algorithm-2025-complete-guide-for-marketers, https://torro.io/blog/instagram-algorithm-2025-explained). I did not see Mosseri's original. (inference) A science story that people want to send to a friend ("look what these creatures did") is doing what Instagram rewards; a surprising, easily retold result helps with that.

### 4.4 Instagram favours original content and penalises reposts [Platform, via trade press]

In April 2024 Instagram changed its ranking to favour original content and smaller original creators. Where it detected a repost, it replaced it in recommendations with the original, and accounts that reposted others' content without meaningfully adding to it lost reach; Mosseri said the penalties target "unoriginal content that [reposters] didn't enhance" (https://techcrunch.com/2024/04/30/instagram-is-updating-its-ranking-systems-to-surface-more-content-from-smaller-original-creators, https://www.engadget.com/instagrams-algorithm-overhaul-will-reward-original-content-and-penalize-aggregators-130018977.html). On 30 April 2026 Tubefilter and PetaPixel reported that aggregator accounts would stop being recommended at all, across photos, carousels and Reels, and summaries say watermarks and credits in the caption do not count as enhancing the source (https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/04/30/instagram-removes-algorithm-recommendations-repost-content-aggregator/, https://petapixel.com/2026/04/30/new-instagram-policies-target-reposted-content/). (inference) The rule is aimed at accounts that repost other people's work; posting your own video natively on each platform keeps clear of it.

### 4.5 Reels with another app's watermark are less discoverable [Platform, via trade press]

In February 2021 Instagram said it would make Reels carrying a TikTok watermark less discoverable in the Reels tab: "content that is visibly recycled from other apps (i.e. contains logos or watermarks) makes the Reels experience less satisfying". Low-resolution video was named as de-prioritised too; followers still see such posts in their feeds. Instagram's guidance at the time also advised against borders and excessive text on the video. https://www.tubefilter.com/2021/02/10/instagram-reels-with-tiktok-watermark-less-discoverable/ and https://wersm.com/instagram-will-deprioritize-content-recycled-from-other-apps/ . (inference) Export a clean master and upload it to each platform separately, never a download from another app.

### 4.6 Most Facebook video was watched without sound: Digiday (2016) [Industry]

**What and who.** "85 percent of Facebook video is watched without sound", Digiday, May 2016. https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/ (also https://www.niemanlab.org/reading/publishers-say-85-percent-of-facebook-video-is-watched-without-sound/)

**Findings.** Publishers reported that as much as 85% of their Facebook video views happened with the sound off. LittleThings, averaging 150 million monthly views on Facebook, had 85% of its viewing without sound; Mic, also averaging 150 million monthly views, said 85% of its 30-second views were silent. A search summary of the coverage also said brand lift and purchase intent did not differ between sound on and sound off.

**Limits.** Two publishers' own numbers, from the Facebook feed in 2016, when feed video autoplayed muted.

**What it means here (inference).** On feeds that autoplay muted, a video has to make sense without sound, which the Reels and TikTok figures do not require to the same degree.

### 4.7 Science on TikTok, the memetic style: Bunquin and Segre Cohen (2026) [Study, from a search summary]

**What and who.** Jon Benedik Bunquin and Alex Segre Cohen, "When scientists 'Tok: Memetic communication and scientific information engagement in short video-based social media", *Public Understanding of Science*, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1177/09636625261469057 . The article page returned HTTP 403, so this entry rests on a search summary.

**Method and findings, as summarised.** A quantitative content analysis of 1,025 science-related videos from 113 TikTok creators. Imitation (following a trend's format) and humour predicted more likes, shares, comments and views, while reappropriation (reusing others' material) predicted fewer. Videos with more views were also liked and shared at higher rates and held viewers longer; hashtags, sounds and effects did not significantly change views.

**Limits.** Not read in full; the terms are the authors' and their definitions were not seen.

**What it means here (inference).** On TikTok, science that plays by the platform's own formats and with some humour does better, and reusing other people's material does worse. Humour is a register the narration could try, provided it does not undercut accuracy.

### 4.8 A second study of science on TikTok, legacy media [Study, title only]

A content analysis in *Analisi* of 9,003 videos published in 2023 by legacy media in Spain and Chile, 326 of them about science, describes the techniques and styles those outlets used on TikTok (https://analisi.cat/article/view/v73-martin-neira-etal). Only the search summary was seen, and it reported no findings on engagement. It is listed so that it is not lost.

### 4.9 LinkedIn and other feeds [unverified]

The figure that 79% of LinkedIn video is watched without sound is repeated by marketing blogs (https://www.opus.pro/blog/linkedin-video-caption-subtitle-best-practices and others); no LinkedIn source was found. The same blogs advise burned-in captions on LinkedIn. No evidence was gathered on X, Bluesky or Facebook Reels. (inference) Treat any feed that autoplays muted like the Facebook feed in 4.6.

### 4.10 Each platform in turn

**YouTube (long-form).** YouTube ranks on clicks, watch time, satisfaction surveys, sharing, likes and dislikes (1.10, 1.11), and shows creators what share of viewers are still watching after 30 seconds, and where they skip or leave (1.5). It removes titles and thumbnails that promise what the video does not deliver (1.12). Among science channels, quick delivery and a regular presenter went with popularity (2.2). No sound-on figure was found, but YouTube is watched mostly as a video site rather than a muted feed. (inference) A longer version of a story belongs here when the story has several turns; the first 30 seconds must deliver the thumbnail's promise.

**YouTube Shorts.** Up to three minutes since October 2024 (3.1). The first metric is whether people stay or swipe away (1.6). Since 31 March 2025 every replay counts as a view and engaged views count for pay (3.2). A practitioner study found 40 seconds or more did well when it held viewers (1.17). (inference) The first second is the thumbnail; 45 to 90 seconds is comfortable.

**TikTok.** TikTok tells advertisers to put the hook in six seconds and the point in three (1.2), and its creator guidance reportedly says five (1.4). Sound is on for most viewers (4.1). Uploads can be ten minutes, but pay requires at least one minute (3.4). Its native, low-polish, people-on-screen style is what TikTok recommends even to advertisers (1.2), and humour and trend formats went with engagement for science creators (4.7). (inference) 60 to 90 seconds if pay matters, narration carrying the story, a less polished and more direct voice than on YouTube.

**Instagram Reels.** Up to three minutes, with longer Reels said to be unrecommended to non-followers (3.3). Mostly sound on (4.2). Ranked on watch time, likes and, for new audiences, shares by message (4.3). Original content only; no other app's watermark (4.4, 4.5). Brand Reels did best at 45 to 60 seconds (3.5). (inference) 45 to 90 seconds, a result people want to send to someone, a clean native upload.

**Facebook feed and LinkedIn.** Largely muted (4.6, 4.9). The first three seconds carry much of what a viewer takes away (1.1). Captions add view time (7.1). (inference) Every point must be readable from the captions and the picture alone.

## 5. Story structure

### 5.1 Stories are understood and remembered better than exposition: Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta (2021) [Study]

**What and who.** R. A. Mar, J. Li, A. T. Nguyen and C. P. Ta, "Memory and comprehension of narrative versus expository texts: A meta-analysis", *Psychonomic Bulletin and Review* 28, 732-749, 2021. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/ and https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33410100/

**Method.** A meta-analysis of more than 75 unique samples, with data from over 33,000 participants, comparing memory and comprehension for narrative texts (stories) with expository texts (essays that explain).

**Findings.** Stories were more easily understood and better remembered than essays. The result was robust: it did not depend on any single study or effect size, and it was not moderated by the study characteristics examined. Before this synthesis the individual studies had disagreed, some favouring stories, some essays, some finding no difference.

**Limits.** Texts, mostly read, not videos; the stories and essays differ in more than structure.

**What it means here (inference).** This is the strongest single piece of evidence that telling an experiment as a story, rather than as a report of results, makes it understood and remembered.

### 5.2 Narratives in science communication: Dahlstrom (2014) [Study, review]

**What and who.** Michael Dahlstrom, "Using narratives and storytelling to communicate science with nonexpert audiences", *PNAS* 111, Supplement 4, 13614-13620, 2014. https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1320645111 and https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25225368/

**Content.** A review. Narratives increase comprehension, interest and engagement. People who are not experts get most of their science from mass media, which already favour narrative. Narratives are intrinsically persuasive, which gives communicators a way to reach resistant audiences but raises ethical questions about using them.

**Limits.** A review of other work, not new evidence.

**What it means here (inference).** Narrative is the form in which the audience already receives science; its persuasive power is a reason to be careful about accuracy, not a reason to avoid it.

### 5.3 Narratives persuade, modestly: Braddock and Dillard (2016) [Study]

**What and who.** Kurt Braddock and James Dillard, "Meta-analytic evidence for the persuasive effect of narratives on beliefs, attitudes, intentions, and behaviors", *Communication Monographs* 83(4), 2016. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03637751.2015.1128555

**Findings.** Exposure to a narrative was positively related to beliefs consistent with the story (37 studies, 7,376 people, r = .17), attitudes (40 studies, 7,132 people, r = .19), intentions (28 studies, 5,211 people, r = .17) and behaviours (5 studies, 978 people, r = .23).

**Limits.** Small to modest effects; persuasion, not understanding.

**What it means here (inference).** A story moves what people believe a little. That is the reason to make sure the belief it moves them towards is the true one.

### 5.4 Being carried into a story: van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti and Wetzels (2014) [Study, scope only]

"The Extended Transportation-Imagery Model: A Meta-Analysis of the Antecedents and Consequences of Consumers' Narrative Transportation", *Journal of Consumer Research* 40(5), 797-817, 2014, reviews two decades of research on "narrative transportation", the experience of mentally entering the world a story evokes, through a meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes from 76 published and unpublished articles. https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/40/5/797/2907487 and https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/ . Only its scope was read, not its findings. It is the standard reference on what makes people get absorbed in a story, if that question needs following up.

### 5.5 More narrative science is cited more: Hillier, Kelly and Klinger (2016) [Study]

**What and who.** A. Hillier, R. P. Kelly and T. Klinger, "Narrative style influences citation frequency in climate change science", *PLoS ONE* 11(12): e0167983, 2016. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0167983 (the PLOS blog's comment: https://theplosblog.plos.org/2016/12/the-importance-of-storytelling-in-science/)

**Method.** 732 abstracts from the climate-change literature, rated for narrative features, compared with how often each article was cited.

**Findings.** Articles with more narrative abstracts were cited more often. The use of sensory language, conjunctions, connectivity and appeal to the reader each correlated significantly with citation frequency. The effect was tied to the journal: higher-impact journals published more narrative articles, and those were cited more.

**Limits.** Correlational and confounded by the journal; scientists reading papers, not the public watching videos.

**What it means here (inference).** Even among experts, writing that connects its steps and appeals to the reader travels further. The finding on conjunctions is the closest thing to evidence for the "and, but, therefore" style of linking.

### 5.6 The common narrative arc: Boyd, Blackburn and Pennebaker (2020) [Study]

**What and who.** Ryan Boyd, Kate Blackburn and James Pennebaker, "The narrative arc: Revealing core narrative structures through text analysis", *Science Advances* 6(32), eaba2196, 7 August 2020. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32821822/ and https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aba2196

**Method.** Computer-based language analysis measured structural and psychological categories of language across about 40,000 traditional narratives (novels, film scripts) and about 20,000 non-traditional ones (science reporting in newspaper articles, TED talks, Supreme Court opinions).

**Findings.** Traditional narratives shared one structure built of three processes. Staging (setting the scene) was highest at the beginning; plot progression rose through the story; and cognitive tension rose and fell around the middle to late parts. No evidence emerged that following this normative structure more closely was related to a story's popularity. Fact-driven texts, including science reporting and TED talks, showed structures that differed from story-based narratives.

**Limits.** Text, not video; popularity measured in the ways the corpora allowed.

**What it means here (inference).** The arc is real and is a good scaffold (set the scene early, build, put the tension in the middle to late part and resolve it), but hitting it more exactly does not make a story more popular. Use it as a shape, not a formula.

### 5.7 Confronting the misconception beats clear exposition: Derek Muller's thesis [Study]

**What and who.** Derek Muller, PhD thesis "Designing effective multimedia for physics education", University of Sydney (research begun in 2004). Muller later founded the Veritasium channel. https://www.veritasium.com/publications , https://www.academia.edu/18111353/PhD_Muller_Designing_effective_multimedia_for_physics_education , and a reader's notes: https://www.bobvanvliet.com/notes/designing-effective-multimedia-for-physics-education/

**Method.** Experiments with physics students watching short multimedia treatments, including an eight-minute video on Newton's first and second laws, in versions that either explained the correct idea clearly or built on dialogue that stated and then confronted the common misconception.

**Findings.** Clear lecture-style videos were not only ineffective; they tended to reinforce students' misconceptions and increase their confidence in the wrong idea. Videos based on dialogue that addressed the misconceptions directly led to better learning. The mental effort students invested was positively related to how much they learned.

**Limits.** Physics students and physics misconceptions; a thesis, summarised here through secondary sources.

**What it means here (inference).** Many viewers come to evolution with a wrong idea (that creatures try to adapt, that evolution aims at something). A story that states the intuitive expectation, shows the simulation breaking it, and then explains, uses exactly this finding.

### 5.8 And, But, Therefore (ABT): Randy Olson [Practitioner]

**What and who.** Randy Olson, a Harvard-trained marine biologist and former professor at the University of New Hampshire who left for filmmaking, developed the ABT template from 2011 and set it out in *Houston, We Have a Narrative: Why Science Needs Story* (University of Chicago Press, 2015). https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/H/bo21174162.html , https://crtc.wustl.edu/and-but-therefore-the-powerful-narrative-framework-transforming-scientific-communication/ , https://www.sesync.org/resources/communications-toolkit-and-therefore-statement , https://ian.umces.edu/blog/more-conversations-with-randy-olson-about-the-use-of-narratives-in-science/

**Content.** First you set the stage with the subject and its context (AND); next you introduce a problem the audience wants to see resolved (BUT); last you give the resolution (THEREFORE). Olson says he took it from the creators of *South Park* and traced it back to the screenwriting teacher Frank Daniel, who identified the elements in a speech in 1986. He measures narrative strength with a "Narrative Index", the ratio of "but" to "and" in a text multiplied by 100, and dullness with an "And Frequency"; his tool treats an And Frequency of 2.5 as optimal and a Narrative Index above 20 as good (http://www.scienceneedsstory.com/blog/the-narrative-index/, https://abtnarrative.com/abt_analyzer.html).

**Limits.** I found no peer-reviewed validation of the template or the index; they are documented in Olson's books and on his site. The closest indirect support is Hillier et al. (5.5) on conjunctions and connectivity.

**What it means here (inference).** The template maps straight onto an experiment: AND (the world and what was changed), BUT (what did not go as expected), THEREFORE (what that shows). It is a check on a script as much as a way to write one: a script with no "but" has no problem to solve.

### 5.9 "Therefore" or "but", never "and then": Trey Parker and Matt Stone [Practitioner]

**What and who.** The creators of *South Park*, speaking to a freshman "Storytelling Strategies" class at NYU's Tisch School of the Arts for MTVU's series "Stand In", 2011. https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2014/03/06/writing-advice-from-south-parks-trey-parker-and-matt-stone/ , https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vGUNqq3jVLg , https://www.washingtonpost.com/blogs/celebritology/post/matt-parker-trey-stone-crash-nyu-storytelling-class-video/2011/09/08/gIQAlbCYCK_blog.html

**Content.** If the words "and then" belong between the beats of a story, you have something boring; between every pair of beats there should be "therefore" or "but". Matt Stone: those words give "the causation between each beat, and that's a story". They say they literally write the beats out to check.

**What it means here (inference).** A test for every script: read each pair of consecutive sentences and ask whether "therefore" or "but" fits between them. Where only "and then" fits, the story has become a list.

### 5.10 The Story Spine: Kenn Adams [Practitioner]

**What and who.** An eight-sentence exercise invented by Kenn Adams, artistic director of Synergy Theater in Walnut Creek, California, in the late 1980s while working with Freestyle Repertory Theatre in New York. The improvisation teacher Rebecca Stockley brought it to Pixar around 1997, and NPR reports that Pixar, Disney and Lucasfilm train incoming writers with it. https://www.npr.org/2026/06/23/nx-s1-5750619/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories and https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/22/the-story-spine-pixars-4th-rule-of-storytelling/

**Content.** "Once upon a time... Every day... But one day... Because of that... Because of that... Because of that... Until finally... And ever since then..."

**What it means here (inference).** For an experiment: once upon a time (the world before the change), every day (what the creatures did), but one day (the change the experiment made), because of that (the chain of consequences the run showed), until finally (the result), and ever since then (what it means, or the next question). It is the same causal chain as ABT, in more steps.

### 5.11 A template for one experiment (inference, built from sections 1 to 5)

The evidence points to one shape. It opens with the hook: in the first one to three seconds, the footage itself shows the surprising outcome or the question it raises (1.1 to 1.9). Then comes the AND: the world, and what this experiment changed. Then the BUT: what went wrong or what nobody expected, where the narration can invite the viewer to guess what happened next (1.8, 5.7). Then the THEREFORE: what the run showed and how we know it, which is the process of the science (6.4). The payoff answers the hook's question exactly as promised (1.10 to 1.13) and ends on the next open question. Every pair of beats should be joined by "but" or "therefore" (5.9), and the edit should slow down at the reveal (2.4).

## 6. Making a science story compelling instead of a list of results

### 6.1 Identifiable individuals draw more sympathy: Small, Loewenstein and Slovic (2007) [Study]

**What and who.** Deborah Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic, "Sympathy and callousness: The impact of deliberative thought on donations to identifiable and statistical victims", *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 102, 143-153, 2007. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597806000057 and https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/SympathyCallous.pdf

**Method.** Field experiments in which people could donate to a child needing help, described either as a single identifiable child or as a statistical group.

**Findings.** People gave more when the victim was one identifiable child than when the need was described statistically. Teaching or priming people to notice this gap had a perverse effect: they gave less to the identifiable victim and no more to the statistical ones, so overall caring and giving fell. Thinking deliberately damped sympathy for the identified victim without creating sympathy for the statistical ones.

**Limits.** Charity decisions about human victims, not interest in a video. The replication record of this line of work is summarised in 6.3.

**What it means here (inference).** A single creature the camera follows is easier to care about than a population graph, even though the population is where the science is.

### 6.2 The effect reaches animals, for some viewers: Markowitz, Slovic, Vastfjall and Hodges (2013) [Study]

**What and who.** Ezra Markowitz, Paul Slovic, Daniel Vastfjall and Sara Hodges, "Compassion fade and the challenge of environmental conservation", *Judgment and Decision Making* 8(4), 397-406, July 2013. https://journal.sjdm.org/13/13321a/jdm13321a.html

**Method and findings.** The first study to ask whether "compassion fade" (compassion falling as the number of victims rises and their identifiability falls) applies to non-human victims. People who did not identify as environmentalists were more willing to donate to a single identifiable endangered animal (a panda or a polar bear) than to a group; environmentalists did not show the effect. Compassion for animals in trouble stayed flat or fell as the scale of the problem grew.

**Limits.** Donations to real endangered species, not interest in simulated creatures.

**What it means here (inference).** A single named animal can work as a protagonist, and it works on exactly the viewers who are not already interested, who are the ones a social video most needs to reach.

### 6.3 The effect is real but small: Lee and Feeley (2016) [Study]

**What and who.** Seyoung Lee and Thomas Hugh Feeley, "The identifiable victim effect: a meta-analytic review", *Social Influence* 11(3), 2016. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15534510.2016.1216891

**Findings.** A random-effects meta-analysis of 41 studies found an overall identifiable-victim effect that was significant but modest, r = .05. It appeared reliably mainly when there was a single identified (or single unidentified) victim, and when the studies included elements such as a photographed child suffering from poverty, bearing little responsibility for their need, together with a request for money.

**What it means here (inference).** Following one creature helps, but only a little; it is a device, not a strategy. The FLoRA replication atlas also has an entry for Small et al. (https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1016/j.obhdp.2006.01.005/), which was not read.

### 6.4 Tell how the knowledge was made: Dahlstrom and Scheufele (2018) [Study, perspective]

**What and who.** Michael Dahlstrom and Dietram Scheufele, "(Escaping) the paradox of scientific storytelling", *PLoS Biology* 16(10): e2006720, October 2018, part of the collection "Confronting Climate Change in the Age of Denial". A perspective article, not an experiment. https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pbio.2006720

**Content.** The paradox: compelling stories can move people to engage with science, but science has no built-in advantage at telling them and competes with storytellers who are not bound by evidence, so how can science stay credible while using a form that is "agnostic to truth"? Stories work because they follow "a character who experiences events over time, often overcoming conflict on the way." People interpret new information by fitting it into narratives they already hold, which runs against how scientists are trained. Since more scientific knowledge does not by itself lead to more acceptance of science, the authors propose telling stories about how scientific knowledge is produced: "Narrative messages can be constructed toward the goal of engaging audiences to understand the process and credibility of scientific reasoning." "Societal buy-in to science's unique claim to producing the best available evidence depends on citizens appreciating the process that underlies science." They warn that "using storytelling to primarily build scientific support through knowledge, attitude, or behavior goals without also engaging scientific reasoning might not help science in the long run", and that "the plural of the word anecdote ... is not data".

**Limits.** An argument by two leading researchers in the field, not a test.

**What it means here (inference).** An experiment in a simulator is a ready-made process story: what was asked, what was changed, what happened that nobody expected, and how it was checked. Telling it that way is both the more compelling story and the more honest one. The warning about anecdotes applies directly: one run, or one creature, is one case.

### 6.5 What spreads: Berger and Milkman (2012) [Study]

**What and who.** Jonah Berger and Katherine Milkman, "What makes online content viral?", *Journal of Marketing Research* 49(2), 192-205, 2012. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmr.10.0353 and https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1528077

**Method.** Every New York Times article published over three months, analysed for the emotions it evoked and compared with whether it made the paper's most-emailed list, plus experiments to test causation.

**Findings.** Virality was partly driven by physiological arousal. Content that evoked high-arousal positive emotion (awe) or high-arousal negative emotion (anger or anxiety) was more viral; content evoking low-arousal emotions such as sadness was less viral. Positive content was more viral than negative. These results held after controlling for how surprising, interesting or practically useful the content was, each of which was itself positively linked to virality, and for how prominently the paper featured it. The experiments showed that the specific emotion had a causal effect, driven by the level of activation it induced.

**Limits.** News articles shared by email, about 2008; sharing, not understanding.

**What it means here (inference).** The emotions naturally available to a simulator story are awe at what emerges from simple rules and surprise at an unexpected result. Both are the high-arousal kind that spreads. Anger and anxiety also spread, but there is no honest route to them here, and there should not be.

### 6.6 Emotion without a narrative frame [Study, weak]

The search that found the background-music meta-analysis (7.14) also turned up a finding that a narrative frame was essential for sustained learning from an instructional video: without one, emotional audiovisual design hindered sustained learning, and with one, it maintained the viewer's situational interest. It came from a search summary of https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1393962.pdf and https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-020-09783-4 , and the specific study was not identified. (inference) If it holds, dramatic music and visuals help only when a story holds them together.

### 6.7 A wrong video feels as clear as a right one: Kulgemeyer and Wittwer (2022) [Study]

**What and who.** Christoph Kulgemeyer and Jorg Wittwer, "Misconceptions in Physics Explainer Videos and the Illusion of Understanding: an Experimental Study", *International Journal of Science and Mathematics Education*, 2022. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8932681/

**Method.** Two nearly identical physics explainer videos, matched in length, visual design, language complexity and teaching quality; one contained common misconceptions and one was scientifically accurate. 149 first-semester trainee teachers were randomly assigned to one of them, and understanding, misconceptions and perceived learning were measured.

**Findings.** The group that watched the misconception video developed more misconceptions and fewer correct conceptions, yet both groups felt equally confident they understood: "Learners from the experimental group developed an equal belief of understanding, but more misconceptions and fewer scientifically correct conceptions than those from the control group." The authors warn that "students who have watched a misleading video might regard further teaching in school as irrelevant", and suggest this illusion of understanding may explain why poor videos with misconceptions can be rated highly on YouTube.

**Limits.** One topic, one sample of trainee teachers.

**What it means here (inference).** Viewers cannot tell a wrong science video from a right one, and will like and share both. The maker is the only check on accuracy; audience response is no guide to it.

### 6.8 Desire language distorts evolution: Legare, Lane and Evans (2013) [Study]

**What and who.** Cristine Legare, Jonathan Lane and E. Margaret Evans, "Anthropomorphizing science: How does it affect the development of evolutionary concepts?", *Merrill-Palmer Quarterly* 59(2), 2013. https://digitalcommons.wayne.edu/mpq/vol59/iss2/4/ and https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5f6be63b8e2a576b0adb4b6d/t/60ca69ddfc8bfe157c039702/1623878111550/anthropomorphizing-science.pdf

**Method.** 88 children aged 5 to 12 heard one of three kinds of narrative about animals changing over generations: need-based (referring to the animals' basic survival needs), desire-based or anthropomorphic (referring to the animals' mental states, what they wanted), or scientifically accurate natural-selection narratives. Their endorsement and use of evolutionary concepts was then measured.

**Findings.** The language used shaped how the children interpreted evolutionary change. Anthropomorphic narratives were the least likely to lead to a scientifically accurate interpretation. Need-based and natural-selection language had similar, positive effects, which the authors suggest means need-based reasoning can be a stepping stone to a selection explanation. The paper notes the wider criticism that compelling but imprecise language about evolution feeds intuitive biases about purpose (teleology) and fixed essences.

**Limits.** Children, not adult viewers.

**What it means here (inference).** Phrases like "the creatures wanted to reach the food" or "they learned to swim" are the kind of language that produced the least accurate understanding. "The ones that happened to swim faster left more offspring" is the kind that works.

### 6.9 In adults too: anthropocentric language and misconceptions (2025) [Study, abstract only]

"Spontaneous Anthropocentric Language Use in University Students' Explanations of Biological Concepts Varies by Topic and Predicts Misconception Agreement", *CBE Life Sciences Education*, 2025, found that university students' spontaneous use of human-centred language when explaining biology varied by topic and predicted how far they agreed with misconceptions. https://www.lifescied.org/doi/10.1187/cbe.24-07-0198 and https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39808720/ . Only the title and abstract summary were seen. It extends the concern in 6.8 from children to adults, though it shows an association, not that the language causes the misconceptions.

### 6.10 A story can teach evolution correctly: Kelemen et al. (2014) [Study]

**What and who.** Deborah Kelemen, Natalie Emmons, Rebecca Seston Schillaci and Patricia Ganea, "Young children can be taught basic natural selection using a picture-storybook intervention", *Psychological Science*, 2014. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24503874/ , https://www.bu.edu/cdl/files/2014/02/Kelemen_et_al_Natural_Selectionplussupp.pdf , https://www.bu.edu/articles/2014/can-young-children-understand-complex-science/

**Method.** A ten-page picture storybook read aloud to children aged five to eight. It tells of "pilosas", fictional mammals with trunks that catch insects. In the past most had wide trunks and a few thin ones; then extreme climate change drove the insects into long, narrow tunnels underground, where only thin-trunked pilosas could reach them.

**Findings.** The children understood the population-based logic of natural selection and generalised it to new cases, one of the hardest steps in learning. The story helped them explain change by natural selection rather than by design or purpose.

**Limits.** Young children and a single, carefully built story.

**What it means here (inference).** A story does not have to trade accuracy for engagement. The pilosa book is a good model for these videos: the variation already exists, the environment changes, the ones that happen to fit survive and breed, and the population shifts. Nobody wants or tries anything.

### 6.11 Interesting but irrelevant material hurts learning: Sundararajan and Adesope (2020) [Study]

**What and who.** Narayankripa Sundararajan and Olusola Adesope, "Keep it Coherent: A Meta-Analysis of the Seductive Details Effect", *Educational Psychology Review*, 2020. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4

**Method.** A meta-analysis of 68 studies on "seductive details": interesting but irrelevant additions to learning material, such as fun facts or decorative images.

**Findings.** Including seductive details lowered learning, g = -0.33, a small to moderate effect. The size of the effect depended on the kind of detail, the kind of image it was compared with, the delivery format, the language, the subject, whether learners controlled the pace, the type of recall question and how the manipulation was checked. The authors' explanation is that the extra material loads working memory and gets in the way of building a coherent mental model. A related study found seductive details hamper learning even when they do not disrupt the flow of the material (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10176302/), and a 2026 study in *Instructional Science* examines how the amount of seductive detail matters (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-026-09781-8).

**Limits.** Learning material, measured by learning; a fun fact may still hold attention even as it costs understanding.

**What it means here (inference).** A striking aside about the simulator, a decorative graphic or a joke that does not serve the story each carry a measurable cost to what the viewer understands. Keep only what moves the story or explains the result.

### 6.12 Science TikTok rarely cites sources: Morais and Fernandes (2026) [Study]

**What and who.** Ricardo Morais and Clara Eloise Fernandes, "Scrolling through science: how accurate is science content on TikTok", *Journal of Science Communication*, 2026. https://jcom.sissa.it/article/pubid/JCOM_2502_2026_A03/

**Method.** Mixed methods: 21 TikTok science creators with more than 100,000 followers, in astronomy, physics, chemistry, biology and engineering, and the last ten videos of each (210 videos), collected from 1 to 9 June 2025 with scraping tools. The authors assessed factual accuracy, scientific soundness, evidence behind claims, transparency about sources and rigour.

**Findings.** "A majority of the videos do not credit verifiable sources", leaving viewers to trust the creator; most lacked citations for images, data and claims, even when they showed the title page of an article. Most creators held relevant degrees or professional backgrounds, but "most have never published scientific or academic works", which the authors think may explain the weak citation habits. Creators who stated their credentials openly fared better, while vague references to unnamed "scientists" undermined credibility without letting anyone check. The paper cites other work saying that "73% of content on platforms like TikTok is inaccurate or overly generalised" yet draws engagement comparable to accurate content, and says the algorithm prioritises engagement over accuracy; it gives no data of its own linking accuracy to engagement.

**Limits.** A small, selected sample; the 73% figure is not the authors' own measurement.

**What it means here (inference).** Stating where the result comes from (a named simulator, its runs, where the data and code live) and who made it is uncommon on science TikTok, and is one way to stand out as credible.

### 6.13 A channel that audits itself: Kurzgesagt (2019) [Practitioner]

**What and who.** "Can You Trust Kurzgesagt Videos?", a six-minute video by the animated science channel Kurzgesagt, 3 March 2019. https://www.imdb.com/title/tt9872608/ and https://www.patreon.com/posts/new-video-can-25098778

**Content.** The channel explained how it researches and writes its videos, with fact-checking and editing by scientists, admitted mistakes and biases, and explained why two of its most popular older videos did not meet its own standards. It publishes its sources for each video.

**What it means here (inference).** A science channel that shows its sources and corrects itself in public gains trust from it. A short sources note or link under each story, and a correction when one is needed, are cheap versions of the same thing.

### 6.14 Labelling synthetic content on YouTube [Platform]

**What and who.** YouTube's help page on disclosing altered or synthetic content. https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491

**Content.** Creators must disclose realistic content made with AI or other tools that makes a real person appear to say or do something they did not, alters footage of a real event or place, or "generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur". Disclosure is not needed for clearly unrealistic content (fantasy scenes, green screen, fully animated videos), for minor edits (beauty filters, colour correction, special effects, upscaling, generated captions, and "cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs"), or for production help (using AI "to create or improve a video outline, script, thumbnail, title, or infographic"). The page says disclosure "won't limit a video's audience or impact its eligibility to earn money".

**What it means here (inference).** Rendered simulation footage is most likely exempt as animation. A synthetic narration voice that is not a clone of a real person does not appear on either list; that is worth checking if the narration is generated. Separately from any rule, a word on screen saying the footage is a simulation costs nothing and stops viewers taking it for real animals.

### 6.15 Accuracy rules for stories about evolved creatures (inference, from 6.4 to 6.14)

Describe what happened in selection language: "the ones that happened to swim faster left more offspring", not "they learned to swim" or "they wanted to reach the food" (6.8, 6.10). Present one run as one run and one creature as one case, and say when other runs went differently (6.4). Keep the population as the unit of the explanation even when the camera follows one individual (6.1 to 6.3). Cut asides that do not serve the explanation (6.11). Say where the result comes from (6.12, 6.13). Remember that the audience cannot catch errors and will reward a wrong video as readily as a right one (6.7).

## 7. Narration, subtitles, on-screen text and graphics

### 7.1 How many watch muted, and what captions do: the Verizon Media and Publicis Media survey, and Facebook's figures [Industry; Platform]

**What and who.** A survey by Verizon Media in partnership with Publicis Media: an online survey of 5,616 US adults aged 18 to 54, April 2019. https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/verizon-media-and-publicis-media-find-viewers-want-captions/ , https://www.forbes.com/sites/tjmccue/2019/07/31/verizon-media-says-69-percent-of-consumers-watching-video-with-sound-off/ , https://rbr.com/video-captions-study/ , https://www.streamingmedia.com/Articles/ReadArticle.aspx?ArticleID=131860

**Findings.** 69% of people view video with the sound off in public places. 92% view with the sound off on mobile and 83% across all devices. "80% say they're more likely to watch an entire video with captions." 50% agree captions are important because they usually watch with the sound off on mobile and desktop, and one viewer in three keeps captions on in public settings. 37% say captions encourage them to turn the sound on out of interest, and 29% keep watching with the sound off because captions help them understand. For ads, open captions lifted ad recall by 8%, ad memory quality by 10% and brand linkage by 13%, and within the first six seconds captions brought a 42% lift in emotional engagement and a 214% lift in perceived uniqueness.

**Facebook's figures** (entry 1.1's page, https://www.facebook.com/business/news/updated-features-for-video-ads): "captioned video ads increase video view time by an average of 12%", and in one study "41% of videos were basically meaningless without sound".

**Limits.** A self-reported survey by media companies, in 2019, before TikTok and Reels made sound-on viewing the norm on those platforms; Facebook's figures are its own, about ads.

**What it means here (inference).** Many people watch muted in public, and captions make people more likely to finish. On sound-on platforms (4.1, 4.2) the captions still help understanding, as 7.3 shows.

### 7.2 Hearing loss worldwide: WHO (2026) [Platform, official statistics]

The World Health Organization's fact sheet on deafness and hearing loss, dated 3 March 2026 (https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/deafness-and-hearing-loss), says: "Over 5% of the world's population - or 430 million people - require rehabilitation to address their disabling hearing loss." By 2050 "over 700 million people - or 1 in every 10 people - will have disabling hearing loss". About 95.1 million children aged 5 to 19 live with hearing loss, and over a billion young adults are at risk of avoidable hearing loss from unsafe listening. (inference) Captions are not only for muted viewers; for about one person in twenty they are the only way to follow the narration.

### 7.3 Captions help everyone: Gernsbacher (2015) [Study, review]

**What and who.** Morton Ann Gernsbacher, "Video Captions Benefit Everyone", *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 2, 195-202, 2015. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5214590/

**Content.** A review. More than 100 empirical studies document that captioning a video improves comprehension of, attention to and memory for the video, for children, adolescents, college students and adults. Captions help most for people watching in a language that is not their first, for children and adults learning to read, and for people who are deaf or hard of hearing.

**Limits.** Mostly educational and language-learning settings; same-language captions shown with sound.

**What it means here (inference).** Captions are worth having even for viewers who hear the narration, and more so for an international audience watching in English as a second language.

### 7.4 Narration plus on-screen text: Adesope and Nesbit (2012) [Study]

**What and who.** Olusola Adesope and John Nesbit, "Verbal redundancy in multimedia learning environments: A meta-analysis", *Journal of Educational Psychology* 104, 250-263, February 2012. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232469670_Verbal_Redundancy_in_Multimedia_Learning_Environments_A_Meta-Analysis and https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2011-25165-001

**Method.** A meta-analysis of 57 independent studies, mostly with university students, comparing learning from spoken-only, written-only and spoken-plus-written presentations.

**Findings.** Spoken-plus-written presentations did not differ from written-only ones, but learners who got spoken-plus-written outperformed those who got spoken-only. That advantage appeared for learners with little prior knowledge, for material presented at a fixed pace (system-paced), and for material without pictures; it depended on whether animations or diagrams were included. Presentations that showed key terms taken from the narration, rather than the full text, were linked to better learning and accounted for much of the advantage. The authors' explanation is that at a fixed pace people are more likely to mishear speech than to misread text, and the text lets them correct what they misheard.

**Limits.** Learning outcomes in instructional settings; "redundancy" in the older multimedia-learning literature (Mayer's redundancy principle) warned against on-screen text with narration and animation, and this meta-analysis shows the picture is conditional.

**What it means here (inference).** These videos are fixed-pace (which favours captions) but full of moving footage (where the advantage shrinks). Short caption chunks, with the key terms emphasised, compete less with the picture than long blocks of text.

### 7.5 Netflix's subtitle standard [Industry, standard]

Netflix's Timed Text Style Guide (https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-General-Requirements and https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/360051554394-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-Subtitle-Timing-Guidelines; summary at https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/netflix-captioning-specs/) sets a maximum reading speed of 20 characters a second for adult programmes and 17 for children's, a maximum of 42 characters a line for most Latin-alphabet languages, a minimum of five-sixths of a second on screen for each subtitle (20 frames at 24 frames a second) and a maximum of seven seconds. It is an industry standard built from long professional practice, not an experiment. (inference) It is a sensible ceiling for burned-in captions: at most 20 characters a second and 42 a line, and nearer 17 if children may watch.

### 7.6 The BBC's subtitle guidelines [Industry, standard, via a secondary source]

As summarised by a captioning company (https://www.clevercast.com/bbc-subtitling-guidelines/; see also https://broadcastwriter.com/2024/12/12/bbc-subtitle-style-guide-2024/), the BBC recommends a subtitle speed of 160 to 180 words a minute, 0.33 to 0.375 seconds a word, with about 38 characters a line on broadcast teletext and about 68% of the frame's width online, roughly 40 to 42 characters. The BBC's own guideline page was not read. (inference) Narration at a normal pace of about 150 to 170 words a minute can be captioned in full within this limit; faster narration will need condensed captions.

### 7.7 Viewers keep up with fast subtitles: Szarkowska and Gerber-Moron (2018) [Study]

**What and who.** Agnieszka Szarkowska and Olivia Gerber-Moron, "Viewers can keep up with fast subtitles: Evidence from eye movements", *PLoS ONE* 13(6): e0199331, June 2018. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0199331

**Method.** 74 English, Polish and Spanish viewers were eye-tracked while watching films subtitled at 12, 16 and 20 characters a second, with attention to whether they were used to subtitles and whether they knew the film's language.

**Findings.** Subtitles have grown longer and faster over the years, from 32 to 42 characters a line and from 12 to 17 to 20 characters a second, and the viewers in the study could keep up with the fast subtitles.

**Limits.** Films on a large screen, not captions on a phone over busy footage.

**What it means here (inference).** Up to 20 characters a second is readable, which supports the Netflix ceiling; there is no reason to slow captions below that, but no evidence for going above it.

### 7.8 TikTok's "5 to 10 words per second" [Platform, treated with caution]

TikTok's ad help page (entry 1.2) advises "displaying 5-10 words per second when using text". That is two to three times the professional standards above: 20 characters a second is about three to four words a second. The page may mean short text overlays of a few words rather than running captions, but it does not say so. I would not use it as a caption speed.

### 7.9 Animated word-by-word captions [unverified vendor claims]

Vendor blogs promote "kinetic" captions that show one word or a short phrase at a time, often with the key word coloured, and claim that captions raise Reels completion from 37% to 65%, that animated formats get 41% higher engagement, and that 78.6% of 13.5 million clips they analysed used animated captions (https://reelwords.ai/blog/animated-captions, https://buildmytribe.io/articles/kinetic-captions-vs-static-captions-high-earner-brands-2026, https://kreateflo.com/blog/why-dynamic-text-animations-outperform-static-subtitles). None of these gives a method, and no peer-reviewed study was found on word-by-word captions. The style is common on short-form platforms; whether it helps or distracts is untested. (inference) The key-terms finding in 7.4 is the nearest evidence, and it suggests emphasising the load-bearing word is sound whatever the animation.

### 7.10 A modern synthetic voice is as good as a human one: Craig and Schroeder (2017) [Study]

**What and who.** Scotty Craig and Noah Schroeder, "Reconsidering the voice effect when learning from a virtual human", *Computers and Education* 114, 193-205, 2017. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131517301653 and https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1016/j.compedu.2017.07.003

**Method.** A randomised pretest-posttest design: learners watched an instructional animation narrated by an older text-to-speech engine (Microsoft's, of early-2000s quality), a modern one (Neospeech) or a recorded human voice.

**Findings.** In most respects the modern engine was not statistically different from the human voice in learners' perceptions, learning outcomes or cognitive efficiency. It produced significantly more learning on transfer questions than the older engine, was more efficient, and was rated as highly as the human voice for helping learning and for credibility. A follow-up by the same authors (2019, *Journal of Educational Computing Research*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0735633118802877) and work on voice and trust (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1071181319631517) continue the line.

**Limits.** An instructional setting and voices of 2017; today's voices are better still.

**What it means here (inference).** The old "voice principle", that a human voice always teaches better than a machine one, does not hold for a good modern voice. What costs credibility is a voice that sounds dated or robotic.

### 7.11 Conversational style helps: Ginns, Martin and Marsh (2013) [Study]

**What and who.** Paul Ginns, Andrew Martin and Herbert Marsh, "Designing instructional text in a conversational style: a meta-analysis", *Educational Psychology Review* 25(4), 445-472, 2013. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-013-9228-0 and https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1036785

**Method.** A meta-analysis of studies that rewrote instructional material in a conversational style: addressing the learner directly (changing first- or third-person text to "you"), adding sentences that speak to the learner, using more polite forms of address, and making the author's views and personality more visible.

**Findings.** The conversational versions improved retention (d = .30) and transfer (d = .54) across a range of moderators. Learners also rated them friendlier (d = .46) and reported more effective processing (d = .45), but not more help with learning (d = .16) or more interest (d = .15).

**Limits.** Mostly written instructional text; the interest measure did not move.

**What it means here (inference).** Narration that speaks to the viewer ("watch this one") and lets a narrator's voice show is supported by the evidence. It helps understanding more than it raises interest.

### 7.12 A human voice and early text in TikTok ads [Platform]

TikTok's 2021 analysis (entry 2.6) found a human voiceover combined with a written offer gave an 87% conversion lift in e-commerce ads, captions or on-screen text an 80% lift, and on-screen text within the first seven seconds a 43% lift in gaming ads. The same caveats apply: ads, conversions, the platform's own analysis. (inference) Voice plus text from the start went with better results even for selling.

### 7.13 Cues that point attention help: Schneider et al. (2018), and a second meta-analysis [Study]

**What and who.** Sascha Schneider, Maik Beege, Steve Nebel and Gunter Daniel Rey, "A meta-analysis of how signaling affects learning with media", *Educational Research Review*, 2018. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X17300581 . A second meta-analysis, Alpizar, Adesope and Wong, "A meta-analysis of signaling principle in multimedia learning environments", *Educational Technology Research and Development*, 2020, is at https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7 (not read in detail).

**Method.** 103 studies with 12,201 participants on "signalling": cues added to learning material (arrows, highlighting, colour, labels, spoken emphasis) to direct attention to the relevant elements.

**Findings.** Signalling improved retention (g = 0.53, 95% confidence interval 0.42 to 0.64) and transfer (g = 0.33, 0.22 to 0.43). It also reduced cognitive load and affected motivation, learning time and eye fixations on the relevant material.

**Limits.** Learning material; the effect on watching was not measured.

**What it means here (inference).** In a frame full of creatures, the viewer needs to be shown which one the narration means. An outline, an arrow, a highlight or a label on the creature being discussed is one of the best-supported techniques in this whole document.

### 7.14 Background music: de la Mora Velasco et al. (2023) [Study]

**What and who.** Efren de la Mora Velasco, Yuting Chen, Atsusi Hirumi and Haiyan Bai, "The impact of background music on learners: A systematic review and meta-analysis", *Psychology of Music*, 2023. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/03057356231153070 . An earlier systematic review, "The effects of background music on learning", *Educational Technology Research and Development*, 2020: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-020-09783-4 . A study of background music in instructional videos: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1393962.pdf . An older meta-analysis on background music and adult listeners (Kampfe et al.): https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/music/distraction/2010-kampfe.pdf

**Findings (from the search summary).** A small positive mean effect of background music on learning (d = 0.314); a medium positive effect when the music came before the learning assessment; small positive effects for factual retention and for classical music compared with other genres. Classical music in a pre-training message significantly improved retention, but it had no notable effect when embedded in the narrated content. The authors say this challenges the multimedia-learning advice to avoid background music, and that age may moderate the effect.

**Limits.** Read through a search summary; learning, not watching; mostly classical music.

**What it means here (inference).** Music is not clearly harmful, but there is no evidence it helps under the narration itself. Keep it well below the voice, and let it carry the opening and the transitions rather than compete with the explanation.

### 7.15 Loud surprises annoy [Platform]

Facebook's page (entry 1.1) reports that "80% react negatively" when feed-based mobile video ads play loudly and unexpectedly. (inference) No sudden loud stings, especially in the opening seconds when the viewer may have just turned the sound on.

### 7.16 Safe zones [Platform]

TikTok asks that content be kept "visible within the UI safe zone as per ad specifications" (entry 1.2), and Meta's Reels guidance mentions a safe zone for key messages without giving its dimensions (entry 4.2). The platforms' buttons, captions and account names sit over the right edge and the bottom of the frame. (inference) Keep captions, labels and the creature being discussed away from the bottom and right edges on vertical versions.

### 7.17 What the narration and caption evidence adds up to

On TikTok and Reels most viewers hear the narration (4.1, 4.2), so it carries the story there; on muted feeds the captions and the picture must carry it alone (4.6, 7.1). Captions help understanding for everyone (7.3), and short chunks with the key terms emphasised compete least with busy footage (7.4). Twenty characters a second and 42 a line is a safe ceiling (7.5 to 7.7). A good modern synthetic voice is as effective as a human one (7.10), and a conversational style helps understanding (7.11). Visual cues pointing at the relevant creature are strongly supported (7.13), decoration is not (6.11), and music should stay under the voice (7.14, 7.15).

## 8. Common mistakes that lose viewers

The first mistake is a slow or branded opening: a logo, a greeting, or context before the question. The platform data say the first three to six seconds decide much of what a viewer takes away and whether they stay (1.1, 1.2, 1.5, 1.6); the specific advice to cut animated intros is practitioner opinion (1.18), but it follows from that data.

The second is a hook the video does not pay off. YouTube moved away from ranking on clicks because clicks did not mean satisfaction, now surveys viewers, and removes the worst cases (1.10 to 1.12, platform), and an experiment suggests clickbait lowers perceived credibility (1.13, study). For science the usual form is overstating the finding in the title or the first line.

The third is reciting results as a list, "and then... and then". Stories are understood and remembered better than exposition (5.1, a meta-analysis), and the "but or therefore" rule (5.9) and ABT (5.8) are the practitioner tools for avoiding it.

The fourth is fast cutting on top of a dramatic moment and an explanation at once, which overloads viewers and lowers memory for the content, more so for older viewers (2.4, study).

The fifth is decoration: fun facts, flashy graphics or effects that do not serve the story, which lower understanding (6.11, a meta-analysis).

The sixth is desire or purpose language about evolution, "they wanted to", "they learned to", "in order to", which produced the least accurate understanding in children (6.8, study) and goes with misconceptions in adults (6.9).

The seventh is confident error, and presenting one run or one creature as the rule. Viewers of a wrong video feel they understood as well as viewers of a right one (6.7, study), and "the plural of anecdote is not data" (6.4, perspective).

The eighth is a video that makes no sense muted on feed platforms, or captions that are too fast or sit under the platform's buttons (4.6, 7.1, 7.5 to 7.7, 7.16; platform and industry).

The ninth is cross-posting a file with another platform's watermark to Reels, or posting material that is not your own (4.4, 4.5, platform).

The tenth is running a Reel past three minutes when the aim is to reach new viewers (3.3, reported), or making a TikTok just under a minute when income matters (3.4).

The eleventh is a dated or robotic synthetic voice. A modern text-to-speech voice is as effective and credible as a human one; an old one is not (7.10, study).

The twelfth is loud or sudden music, or music that competes with the narration (7.14, 7.15; the music evidence is mixed).

The thirteenth is giving no sources or credentials for the claims, which is common on science TikTok and undermines credibility (6.12, a small study).

## 9. Evidence versus opinion, in one place

**Solid evidence**, from meta-analyses or large studies, though none of it on short social video: stories are understood and remembered better than exposition (5.1); narratives shift beliefs and attitudes modestly (5.3); captions improve comprehension, attention and memory (7.3); narration with on-screen key terms beats narration alone, with conditions about pictures and prior knowledge (7.4); cues that point attention help (7.13) and irrelevant decoration hurts (6.11); a conversational style helps understanding (7.11); the identifiable-individual effect is real but small (6.3).

**Single good studies**: curiosity improves memory (1.7); prediction followed by surprise improves learning (1.8); a video with misconceptions creates false confidence (6.7); desire language distorts understanding of evolution (6.8), while a selection story teaches it (6.10); a modern synthetic voice matches a human one (7.10); fast pacing with arousing content overloads memory (2.4); engagement with course videos falls off beyond about six minutes and follows the speaker's energy (2.1); popular science YouTube goes with quick delivery and a regular host (2.2); awe, surprise and interest make content spread (6.5); more narrative science abstracts are cited more (5.5); stories share an arc but following it more closely does not predict popularity (5.6); confronting misconceptions teaches better than clear exposition (5.7).

**Mixed evidence**: giving away the ending (1.9); background music (7.14).

**Platform data**, self-interested and mostly about ads: the first three to six seconds carry much of an ad's effect (1.1, 1.2); captions add view time (7.1); ads of 21 to 34 seconds converted best and varied scenes helped (2.6); sound is on for most TikTok and Reels viewing and off for much feed viewing (4.1, 4.2); ranking uses watch time and satisfaction, not clicks alone (1.10, 1.11).

**Industry data**: brand Reels of 45 to 60 seconds did best in 2026 (3.5); marketing videos under a minute average 52% engagement and educational ones of one to five minutes hold over half (3.6); the 2019 caption survey (7.1); publishers' 2016 muted-viewing figures (4.6).

**Opinion (practitioner)**: ABT, "but or therefore" and the Story Spine as formulas (5.8 to 5.10); opening on a mystery (1.14); a visual change every few seconds and "rehooks" (2.7); hooks under two seconds and the first second as a thumbnail (1.17, 0.3); loop endings (3.2); animated word-by-word captions (7.9); swipe-away benchmarks (0.3); cutting branded intros (1.18); "crazy progression" (1.16).

## 10. Gaps

No study was found that tests story structure, hook type or length against retention for short narrated science videos. The closest are Habibi and Salim (3.8, one account) and Welbourne and Grant (2.2, YouTube, correlational). No study was found on narrated animal or creature footage without a human presenter, so whether a followed creature can stand in for a host is untested; that is inference only (6.1 to 6.3). No peer-reviewed study was found on animated word-by-word captions, loop endings or visual changes at a set interval. Most platform numbers are about ads and are published by the platforms themselves. Several widely quoted figures could not be checked at their source (0.3). Among sources found but not read in full are van Laer et al. on narrative transportation (5.4), Bunquin and Segre Cohen on science TikTok (4.7), the *Analisi* study of legacy media on TikTok (4.8) and Alpizar et al. on signalling (7.13); any of them could be followed up.

## 11. The ten most useful, best-supported principles for these videos

1. **Show the puzzle in the first one to three seconds, in the footage itself:** the strange outcome, or the question it raises, not a title card. The curiosity study (1.7) and the platforms' first-seconds data (1.1, 1.2, 1.6) support it.

2. **Make the hook a promise the video keeps.** The platforms rank on satisfaction as well as clicks and remove egregious clickbait, and overselling costs credibility (1.10 to 1.13).

3. **Tell one experiment as a causal chain, And, But, Therefore, never as a list of results.** Stories are understood and remembered better (5.1); ABT and "but or therefore" are the practitioner tools (5.8, 5.9).

4. **Tell the process:** what was asked, what was changed, what surprised, and how you know. It keeps the story honest and builds trust in the science (6.4).

5. **Give the story a protagonist, but let the population carry the explanation.** Follow one creature or lineage, knowing the identifiable-individual effect is small (6.1 to 6.3), and keep a regular narrating voice across the series (2.2).

6. **Use surprise deliberately.** Invite the viewer to guess before the reveal, since a broken guess is when learning happens (1.8), and lead with awe and surprise, the emotions that spread (6.5).

7. **Describe evolution in selection language, not wanting language, and never let one run stand in for the result** (6.7, 6.8, 6.10, 6.15).

8. **Make it work both muted and with sound.** The narration is the main channel on TikTok and Reels (4.1, 4.2). Captions go in short chunks, at no more than about 20 characters a second and 42 a line, with the key terms emphasised and clear of the platform's buttons (7.3 to 7.7, 7.16). Label the footage as a simulation (6.14).

9. **Every graphic must serve the story.** Point at the creature the narration means with an outline, arrow or label (7.13); cut decoration, fun facts and asides (6.11); keep music under the voice (7.14).

10. **Fit the length to one idea and to the platform, and slow the edit where the explanation lands.** About 45 to 90 seconds for Shorts, Reels and TikTok: under three minutes on Reels for new viewers, at least a minute on TikTok if income matters. A longer YouTube version only when the story has more turns (section 3). Cut briskly through the setting and hold the shot at the reveal (2.4).
