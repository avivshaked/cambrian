# Why real plants grip: a survey for the forest work

A Sonnet research agent wrote this survey on 2026-11-07 at the coordinator's request, after the owner asked what
motivates a real plant to hold the bottom. It read web pages only and downloaded nothing. **Most sources were read at
abstract or search-summary level, not in full**, and the agent marks where a point is its own general knowledge
("uncited"). Nothing here has gone through the literature review's protocol (`research/LITERATURE-REVIEW.md` §3.5), so
no claim below may be cited in DESIGN.md until its source is read in full. The owner's question, the sibling test that
answered half of it, and what the coordinator draws from both are in `grip-read/` and the forest proposal that follows
this survey.

## 1. Benefits of attachment

**Staying in lit, shallow water.** Light controls where macroalgae can live.
- Desmond, Pritchard & Hepburn (2015), "Light Limitation within Southern New Zealand Kelp Forest Communities", PLoS ONE
  10(4): e0123676, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4406696. Forested sites gave 2 to 5 times more macroalgal
  biomass than the modified coast. Long-term light dose best explained the difference, and nutrients were similar.
- Steneck et al. (2002), "Kelp forest ecosystems: biodiversity, stability, resilience and future", Environmental
  Conservation 29(4): 436-459, https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70242834. Kelp is limited by light at high latitudes,
  and by nutrients, warm water and competing plants at low latitudes.
- A teaching page (Maricopa CC biology notes, secondary) puts the kelps where enough light reaches the bottom, typically
  20 to 30 m in clear water.

**Nutrient supply.** Hurd (2000), "Water motion, marine macroalgal physiology, and production", J. Phycology 36:
453-472. Flow thins the boundary layer round a blade, so it sets how fast nutrients arrive, and production tends to be
higher in wave-exposed habitats. That a fixed plant sits in a steady flow of new water is the agent's inference. A
giant kelp review describes it as held to shallow reefs with seasonal upwelling by light, nutrients and hard substrate.
Seagrasses take nitrogen and phosphorus from both the sediment and the water (a review's abstract, not read in full).

**Resisting being carried away.** No source tested drifting into deep water as a cost of drifting (uncited). Burnett &
Koehl (2022), "Ecological biomechanics of damage to macroalgae", Frontiers in Plant Science 13,
https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.981904/full, treat a holdfast's failure as the lethal mode.

**Recruitment near the parent** (abstracts only). Reed et al. (1988), Ecological Monographs: kelp recruitment fell off
fast with distance from the adult stand, and was significantly lower at only 3 m. Episodic winter storms widened
dispersal, and Reed et al. (2004) found recruits 3.5 km from the nearest source. Gaylord, Reed, Raimondi & Washburn
(2006), Ecological Monographs, modelled release rates above 10^8 spores per plant per day.

## 2. Costs of attachment

- **Dislodgement and drag** (Burnett & Koehl 2022). Drag grows with flow speed, area and shape. Dense canopies shield
  their interior plants. Some perennials build strong thalli at the price of slower growth and later breeding, and some
  weak designs persist by regrowing from a perennial holdfast.
- **Strength against streamlining.** A study of eight kelp species found kelps strong or streamlined but not both
  (secondary listing, authors not retrieved).
- **Seasonal shedding lowers drag.** A two-year field study found low, steady dislodgement despite winter waves, and a
  model put it down to seasonal loss of biomass (PubMed 26236880, title and authors unconfirmed).
- **Shading by neighbours.** Reed & Foster (1984), "The effects of canopy shadings on algal recruitment and growth in a
  giant kelp forest", Ecology 65: 937-948. A canopy cuts bottom light to a small fraction of the surface's, and
  understory recruits appeared only where it was removed. A Santa Barbara study found 10 % of surface light at 1 m
  under a dense canopy, and flow in the bed at 25 % of the incident flow.
- **Construction cost.** That a holdfast is carbon with no photosynthetic return is uncited; the only evidence found is
  Burnett & Koehl's trade-off of strength against growth.

## 3. Growing tall

- **The canopy competes for light** (Reed & Foster 1984): canopy formers shade out what grows beneath them, and a few
  perennials suppress recruitment at sites with little disturbance.
- **Fronds grow up to the surface and form a canopy there.** Baja California Macrocystis fronds averaged about 12.8 cm a
  day in summer and 20.8 in winter (Ciencias Marinas 1996), and growth was nutrient-limited in summer in New Zealand.
- **Floats and phototropism.** No source was found on how floats work, or on growth directed toward light. That gas
  floats hold fronds at the surface, and that growth points up because the float and the growing tip do, are uncited.
- **Translocation.** Lobban (1978), "Translocation of 14C in Macrocystis pyrifera", Plant Physiology 61: 585-589
  (abstract). Mature blades export toward the growing apex and later down to the base and to young fronds. Growing
  tissue imports, and a young frond imports from older ones until it is about 3 m long. Nitrogen moved within large
  fronds but not between them, and the holdfast's nitrogen did not support depleted fronds (abstracts).

## 4. The alternative: drifting

- **Holopelagic Sargassum** lives its whole life afloat, kept up by gas vesicles. It doubled in about 18 to 83 days in
  the open sea, often limited by phosphorus. Wang et al. (2019), "The great Atlantic Sargassum belt", Science, was not
  read for detail. A Florida comparison found pelagic species at least as productive as benthic ones.
- **Rafting kelp** floats, grows and breeds for long periods. Durvillaea rafted from the sub-Antarctic to mainland New
  Zealand in about four weeks after a 2018 storm. Fraser et al. (2020), J. Phycology, found buoyancy selected both for,
  through dispersal, and against, because it narrows the range a plant can live in.
- **Phytoplankton** (Sverdrup's critical depth, a secondary source): mixed deeper than the depth where production
  balances loss, a population declines.
- **When drifting wins** is not tested in any source read. Uncited: where no lit hard bottom exists, as in the open sea,
  and where mixing or rafting keeps the plants lit.

## 5. How a forest feeds its grazers

- **Detritus.** Krumhansl & Scheibling (2012), "Production and fate of kelp detritus", Mar. Ecol. Prog. Ser. 467:
  281-302 (abstract). Kelps shed about 706 g C per m² a year as detritus, about 82 % of their production, most of it in
  storms and after grazers weaken the blades. It feeds beds, shores and the deep sea, from tens of metres to hundreds of
  kilometres away.
- **Drift-feeding against active grazing.** Rennick, DiFiore, Curtis, Reed & Stier (2022), "Detrital supply suppresses
  deforestation to maintain healthy kelp forest ecosystems", Ecology 103(5): e3673 (abstract and press). Plentiful
  detritus keeps urchins feeding passively, and scarce detritus turns them to grazing living kelp. Where grazing
  outstripped kelp production, urchins cut giant kelp biomass fiftyfold. Harrold & Reed (1985), Ecology 66: 1160-1169,
  is the origin of the drift-feeding idea. A Tasmanian time-lapse study found urchins in kelp beds ate drift and almost
  no attached algae, and in barrens ate both.
- **Barrens are a second stable state.** Filbee-Dexter & Scheibling (2014), Mar. Ecol. Prog. Ser. 495: 1-25: shifts in
  and out of barrens happen at different thresholds. Miller, Blain & Shears (2022), Front. Mar. Sci. 9: 831001, citing
  Ling et al. (2015): about 668 g/m² of urchins tips a forest to barren, and about 71 g/m² lets it recover. Barrens can
  last decades.
- **Steadying the grazers.** That the detritus stream keeps grazers fed without killing the living plants is the
  agent's synthesis of Rennick et al. and Krumhansl & Scheibling, not a measured result.

## 6. What limits a forest

Hard substrate, since a recruit needs its holdfast on rock; light, which sets the lower depth limit (Steneck et al.
2002; Desmond et al. 2015; MarLIN's page on Laminaria hyperborea); nutrients at low latitudes; grazing; the canopy's own
shade; and waves.

## 7. Overwintering

- **Arctic Laminaria solidungula** (Dunton & Schell 1986, Mar. Ecol. Prog. Ser. 31: 57-66, via summaries) does over
  90 % of its yearly growth in about nine months of darkness under ice, on reserves built the summer before, and a plant
  that spends them grows less the next year.
- **Laminaria hyperborea** (Sjøtun 1996; MarLIN) stores carbon in summer and autumn and moves it to the meristem for
  late-winter growth. A new blade forms at the base of the old in winter, and the old blade is shed in spring.
- **Seagrass** builds rhizome reserves to a peak by autumn, and autumn shading can spend them before winter.

## What the agent found most useful for the simulation

1. Detritus as a separate food flow that grazers eat without killing the plant, with grazers turning to living plants
   only when drift is scarce.
2. Grazing collapse as a threshold with hysteresis.
3. Attachment limited by hard substrate, with recruitment within metres of the parent and rare long jumps.
4. A tall canopy that pays by shading competitors.
5. Winter survival on stored carbon, and shedding mass to survive storms.

The agent did not verify the Wang et al. (2019) figures, the Reed et al. (1988) numbers, or the strength trade-off's
authors at full text.
