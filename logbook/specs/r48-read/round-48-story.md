# Round 48: the eaters came and went, and a leaf grew a stomach it hardly uses

*The story behind round 48's film, written for a reader who has not seen the project. Every number is read from the three runs; what is interpretation says so. The shot list is `story.json` beside this file, and every caption's source is in `checks.tsv`.*

Round 48 changed four rules to give eaters of dead matter a better chance, then ran the same world three times. In none of the three tanks did a line of eaters last. Eaters arriving from our stock now lived about five minutes instead of under one, and they bred. Their lines still died out within a few thousand seconds. What the round did get is narrower. Late in the first tank, a leaf budded a speck of stomach and founded a line of 38, which meets one prediction to the letter. That stomach is about 0.2% of each body and brings in about 0.2% of its food. The second tank stopped on an error at 13,700 s. The first and third ran to 30,000 s.

## The tank lives on light, and the dead fall as snow

Each world is a disc of water 167 m across and 45 m deep, lit from above. Fifteen to twenty-one flat rock tables stand on stems just under the surface and cover a quarter of its area.

A creature grows from a genome, a recipe for both its body and its small brain. A leaf part earns energy from light. A stomach part earns it by absorbing the dead matter drifting through the water, which we call snow. A link is a plain part that a joint can swing. A body feeds, pays upkeep, grows, breeds once it has saved enough, ages, and dies when its reserve runs out. Its corpse decays into snow, which sinks at two millimetres a second and slowly dissolves back into the water. The leaves need that dissolved matter to build with. The tank starts with 15,000 units of it, and nothing new arrives except the newcomers' starting gifts.

For the first 3,000 s a population floor adds random founders whenever the count runs low. After that a trickle adds one random newcomer every 30 s. One in ten of those is drawn from a pool of four stomach bodies we stored from earlier rounds.

## The round wanted an eater's line

Round 47 asked why stomachs never evolve and found four reasons (logbook/0118). The owner ruled on each, and round 48 runs with all four rulings on (logbook/0119, decisions D119 to D122):

1. A new organ arrives only as a bud. A leaf can grow a small part of another kind and keep working. Before, a whole leaf could turn into a stomach and lose its income in one birth.
2. A child costs in proportion to its size: twice its tissue, with a 50 J floor, where every round since 41 charged 100 J flat. A new gene also lets a line pay as it goes, banking a share of each income into an account it breeds from, which we call gestation.
3. Old age raises upkeep and no longer cuts intake.
4. Every newcomer lands at the richest cell of its food in its column, with 600 s of its own running costs as a starting gift.

The two central predictions were about lines with ten living members in at least one tank. One would start from a stomach budded onto a plant, the other from a pool newcomer.

## Every tank bloomed, then died back

The second tank reached 3,527 bodies at 1,320 s and fell to 2,358 by 4,630 s. The first peaked at 1,830 and fell by a fifth, the third at 1,709 and fell by 44%. At 2,500 s every one of the first tank's 1,776 bodies carried a leaf.

Joints were common at the start, with 377 jointed bodies in the first tank at 2,500 s and 987 in the third at 1,500 s. In two tanks the line that went on to hold the water began as a child that dropped its parent's joint in the first two and a half minutes. In the second tank that was Lamina taplenegax, born at 118.5 s to a jointed leaf of Pinnifrons plisitricrens. In the third it was Thallus legatribens, born at 139 s to a jointed leaf of Natophyllum nocrebax.

## The eaters arrived well placed, and died out anyway

In the third tank, at 3,343.5 s, body 3318 arrived from the pool, a single round stomach with no leaf, carrying a 72 J gift. Its line is Gastrella cidutis. Its first child was born half a second later. The likeliest reading is that the gift paid for it, though I have not checked the account. At 3,500 s it was eating 1.25 W against 0.50 W of upkeep. Its line grew to 26 at 4,049 s, and that tank never held more leafless bodies than the 39 it had at 4,310 s. Then the line shrank to 15 at 5,000 s, 3 at 6,000 s and one at 6,500 s. That last body, 3559, was 2,655 s old, eating 0.55 W, paying 0.73 W and holding 9 J. It starved 44.5 s later and the line ended.

The first tank drew the same stored stomach at 4,057 s, as body 4185, and it too bred half a second after landing. The guide numbers lines that share a founder's genome in the order they were founded, so there this line is Gastrella cidutis III. It grew to 45 at 5,910 s, the largest line of pure eaters in any tank this round. The founder died at 5,926 s, aged 1,869 s, and the last of its line at 8,214 s. Beside it ran Natogastrum plocrele, a stomach with a jointed link, founded by a trickle newcomer at 3,462.5 s. It was the first jointed eater line in that tank to reach ten members, peaked at 14 and was gone by 10,912 s.

Across the tanks, pool newcomers lived a median of 312, 298 and 335 s, where round 47's lived 46, 24 and 42 s. In the three tanks 30, 8 and 18 of them bred, and 54 of those 56 breeders were the one-part sphere. When the first two tanks ended, the only living bodies descended from the pool were pool newcomers themselves, and the third ended with none. The second tank never had a pure eater line of more than two.

I don't know why the lines faded. Body 3318 fed on snow at 1.39 J/m³ when it was young. Body 3559 fed at 0.61 J/m³ when it was old, and paid half as much upkeep again. My guess, from two bodies and not from a measurement across the lines, is that the crowd thinned the snow around itself while its members aged into higher costs. The placing rule also read weaker than intended. Only 19%, 21% and 19% of pool newcomers first fed at or above their column's mean snow. Their median first reading (0.22, 0.25 and 0.18 J/m³) was still higher than round 47's 0.12.

## Two leaves bet on opposite children

In the first tank the long middle belonged to two leaf lines founded 1.5 s apart. Frondium marufubens made two large children at a time, putting a median 91% of an adult body into the pair. Frondium nisimocrax made one child at 7%. At 10,000 s they held 1,315 and 1,386 bodies, nearly half the tank each. Marufubens peaked at 1,439 at 11,470 s and then fell to 829 at 15,000 s, 156 at 20,000 s and 2 at the end. Nisimocrax ended with 8,461 of the 8,618 bodies alive. The tank's mean investment in a child fell from 0.50 of an adult at 10,000 s to 0.077 at 30,000 s.

The third tank ran the same contest the other way. Its big-child line, Thallus legatribens, put a median 68% of an adult into one child and ended with 6,165 of 8,589 bodies. Its small-child line, Thallus migugis, put about 11% into one and ended with 2,340. It had more than doubled in the last 3,500 s, from 1,021 at 26,500 s. I don't know why the same pair of bets ended in opposite leaders. In both tanks the leader at the end already led at 2,500 s, by 773 to 134 in the first and 280 to 30 in the third. So my guess is that an early lead mattered more than the bet.

Gestation entered every tank and was selected out of every tank. Between the two windows the reader compares, the share of children carrying the gestation gene fell from 27% to 13% in the first tank. It fell from 19% to 17% in the second and from 20% to 12% in the third. In the first tank's last 5,000 s a gestating line made 0.47 births per death, and a line that breeds from its reserve made 1.13.

## The second tank stopped at 13,700 s

The second tank kept its joints, with 665 jointed bodies at 12,500 s when the first had 3. Lamina taplenegax, the line that had dropped the joint, held 44% of the tank. At 8,577 s one of its children got a joint back. That child founded Remiphylla pototrapa, which lived at a median depth of 10 cm and reached 187 by the last row. The run stopped at 13,700 s, when the recorder refused a body's snow density of minus infinity. Its last good row is at 13,690 s, and the cause is not known yet.

## A leaf grew a stomach late in the first tank

The buds were born and grown as the rule intended. The first tank had 987 births carrying a bud, and 986 of them grew it. The second had 252 of 301 and the third 481 of 491. Leaves of Frondium nisimocrax budded a stomach 149 times, starting at 1,246 s. Ninety of those lines never grew past their first body, and of the 118 that ended, half lasted less than 1,115 s.

At 23,300.5 s leaf 44820 budded body 48048. It lived 5,854.5 s, where the average body at 27,500 s was 1,894 s old, and had six children. Its line had 38 living members at the end, 29 still carrying the stomach and 9 that had shed it. Two other budded lines also ended with ten or more (31 and 10). The third tank's best budded line ended with 6, so the prediction rests on the first tank alone.

The stomach is 0.17% of a median member's body and brings in 0.24% of its income. Over its life body 48048 earned 0.13% of its energy through it. I read the line as a leaf carrying a stomach that costs it almost nothing. That would explain why selection has not removed it, though it is a guess. The tank has 121 bodies with a stomach at the end, and at least 118 of them are leaves like this.

## The verdict

Read each row for the three tanks against what the clause needed. Seed 2's column is at 13,690 s, against round 47's seed 2 at 30,000 s, and the other two are at 30,000 s.

The round got its buds, its smaller children and its bigger crowd, and in one tank a stomach on a plant that founded a line. In the two tanks that ran to the end, the old lived longer. The round did not get what the rules were for, an eater that lives on snow and leaves descendants. Its eaters now arrive well, live minutes and breed, and their lines fade within a few thousand seconds. Gestation was tried and dropped. The second tank's crash breaks the promise that nothing breaks.
