## What it implies for the world (inference)

- **Release follows eating.** Animals release a fifth to a third of what they eat, and on reefs that release is
  the main nutrient supply. A rule in which nothing releases while alive goes against every consumer source.
  A rule by cell type has no direct basis: release follows diet and metabolism, not tissue. Of the three forms
  of D206 put to the owner, A (a body that eats releases; a body that cannot holds) is closest.
- Plants do leak a little: kelp exudes about 14%. But most of a plant's nutrients leave with its shed tissue
  or at its death, and a plant that holds its matter until the corpse comes close to that.
- A grazer helps its own oasis only weakly. Reef hotspots come mostly from carnivores and from fish
  sheltering in one place, and excretion lands where an animal rests rather than where it feeds. The stronger effect is
  translocation: a grazer that eats in one oasis and releases in another carries matter to the second, which
  rewards migration.
- **Grazers may be priced wrong.** Real herbivores convert plant food at 10 to 30%. The world's grazer economy
  has not been read in those terms.

## Ideas not yet on the table (inference)

1. Release where the body rests rather than where it feeds, so that shelters become oases.
2. Release set by the gap between what the food carries and what the body needs, so that predators fertilise
   most, as on reefs.
3. Sloppy feeding: a kill spills part of its prey at the spot.
4. Matter could age. A corpse would lose its food value to microbes within days, rewarding scavengers that
   arrive quickly. A refractory share would decay very slowly and act as a sink against the fountains. A
   quality per unit (its carbon-to-nutrient ratio) would give creatures something to choose between. The
   scan's reading is that the owner's entropy is better modelled as freshness and composition than as one
   number.

## Hybrids: creatures that both photosynthesise and eat

*A second scan the same day, after the owner asked how hybrids handle their matter. Only Mitra 2016 and
Hartmann 2012 were read in full.*

| Question | Finding | Source | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| How common are they? | Mixotrophs, plankton that both photosynthesise and eat, often dominate the eukaryotic biomass of sunlit water. Each species sits on a sliding scale between the two, set by light and nutrients. In the gyres, production would be much lower without them, through nitrogen and phosphorus starvation. | Mitra et al. 2016, *Protist* 167:106, p.112–116 | full |
| | Algae carrying chloroplasts did 40 to 95% of the eating of bacteria in the summer North Atlantic, and 60 to 77% across 40°N to 40°S. They seem to eat for nutrients, not for carbon. | Zubkov & Tarran 2008, *Nature* 455:224; Hartmann et al. 2012, *PNAS* 109:5756 | abstract; full |
| Do they release what they eat? | A pure eater (*Spumella*) released P and ammonium while eating. A mixotroph (*Ochromonas*) released them only in the dark or with abundant prey; under P limitation it kept the eaten P for its own growth. | Rothhaupt 1997, *Aquat. Microb. Ecol.* 12:65 | abstract |
| | Food webs dominated by mixotrophs return regenerated nutrients to production by a shorter route. | Mitra et al. 2014, *Biogeosciences* 11:995 | abstract |
| Corals | Corals with symbionts excrete less ammonia than one without, and burn their symbionts' fats and sugars to spare nitrogen. The finding is contested. | Szmant et al. 1990, *Mar. Biol.* 104:119 | abstract |
| | The symbionts' uptake of dissolved nutrients can carry fast coral growth, with the host "farming" and digesting surplus symbionts. The authors offer this as the answer to why reefs thrive in poor water. | Wiedenmann et al. 2023, *Nature* 620:1018 | press release |
| | Feeding meets 15 to 35% of a healthy coral's daily needs, and up to 100% of a bleached one's. | Houlbrèque & Ferrier-Pagès 2009, *Biol. Rev.* 84:1 | abstract |
| Stolen chloroplasts | *Mesodinium rubrum* takes over 95% of its carbon from stolen chloroplasts. Sea slugs that keep stolen chloroplasts survive months of darkness as if the chloroplasts were a slowly digested food store. | Johnson et al. 2007, *Nature* 445:426; Christa et al. 2014, *Proc. R. Soc. B* 281 | abstract |

What it implies (inference): in every case a hybrid releases less than a pure eater, and often less than either
pure form, because its leaves take up the nutrients its mouth frees. Release rises when the light side is idle:
in the dark, with abundant prey, or in a bleached coral. A rule that ties release to the share of leaf against
mouth tissue misses this. A rule in which a body's own leaves absorb what it frees, up to their uptake at the
present light, before any reaches the water, follows it.
